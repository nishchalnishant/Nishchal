    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Trading Psychology · module A, chapters 1–7 (your brain, loss aversion, FOMO and the herd, discipline, journal, emotional intelligence, compulsive trading)

    Ravi broke a ₹10,000-a-month SIP habit to put ₹1.5 lakh into a hot small cap, held through a 20% drop, then sold near the low. You can't delete the emotions; build rules that decide before they arrive. Investors are bitten hardest by fear, stock traders by FOMO, F&O buyers by greed, option sellers by hope.

    Check yourself 2 questions

      Show answer

    2. Which emotion bites long-term investors hardest, according to the course?

    Takeaway Instincts built for survival (flee, follow the herd, grab now) work against markets, so pre-decided rules must act before emotions arrive.

    Loss aversion and revenge trading#

    • A loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain pleases. That bends trading backwards: winners cut early, losers held too long.
    • A revenge trade comes right after a loss: bigger, sloppier, no setup, aimed at "getting it back". Poker calls the state tilt, and the urge is strongest exactly when judgement is worst.
    • Meera's planned ₹4,000 stop-out became ₹9,000, then ₹38,000 by 11:30 am: more than two months of losses, on an ordinary market day.
    1. Hard daily loss limit set before the open (illustration: 3% of ₹1 lakh = ₹3,000), then screen off.
    2. A cool-down after any loss that stings: 15 minutes to the rest of the session.
    3. Physically step away: you can't revenge-trade a chart you can't reach.
    4. Test: would I take this exact trade if the last one had won? If not, skip it.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Roughly how much more does a loss hurt than an equal gain pleases?

      Show answer

      About twice as much.

    2. Name two concrete defences against tilt.

      Show answer

      Any two of: a hard daily loss limit set before the open, a cool-down after a stinging loss, physically stepping away, the 'would I take it after a win?' test.

    Takeaway A loss hurts about twice as much as an equal gain pleases, which drives revenge trades; a hard daily limit and a cool-down break the loop.

    FOMO, overconfidence and the herd#

    • FOMO is buying because the price already rose, not because you expect it to rise from here. Arjun put three months of savings into a stock that had tripled; it peaked the next week and halved in two months, while early buyers sold into him.
    • Overconfidence: a few wins, often in an easy rising market (2020–21), feel like skill, so size goes up just before conditions get harder. Keep size fixed by rule, not mood.
    • Herd: when the only reason left to buy is that everyone is buying, the fuel is gone. Unanimous agreement is a warning: who is left to sell to?
    • Luck vs skill: could you write the reason before entry, is it a repeatable rule, did the whole market just rise, would it work on 20 other trades?
    • Don't just flip the crowd either: have your own reason. Missing a winner costs nothing you owned; chasing one can.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What's the difference between FOMO and a real reason to buy?

      Show answer

      FOMO buys because the price already rose; a real reason expects it to rise from here, written before entry.

    2. Why is unanimous agreement a warning sign?

      Show answer

      If everyone has already bought, there's no one left to buy from you: the fuel is gone.

    Takeaway Buy for a reason about the future, not because price already rose; keep size fixed by rule because a few wins in an easy market feel like skill.

    Discipline is a system, not willpower#

    • In the heat of a loss the emotional brain wins the argument. Move the decision to a calm moment and lock it in: pre-commitment. "You do not rise to your intentions; you fall to your systems."
    • Build it in an afternoon: written entry, exit, stop and target; size fixed by rule (≈ 1% risk); a pre-trade checklist; price alerts instead of screen-staring; a journal.
    • A hard stop placed with the order is a default; a mental stop is a hope. Add friction so the wrong action takes several deliberate steps.
    • Rehearse the routine in OpenAlgo's analyzer (sandbox) mode until it's automatic.
    • What to systematise first: investor, an automatic SIP and a "do not sell unless" rule; active trader, a daily loss limit and checklist; F&O beginner, fixed small size and a hard stop; option seller, alerts and a pre-defined exit.
    A system you can override in one click, or a checklist you lie on, is theatre. And no discipline fixes a strategy with no edge.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What is pre-commitment?

      Show answer

      Making the decision in a calm moment and locking it in (written rules, a hard stop with the order) so the heated you can't easily override it.

    2. Why is a mental stop weaker than a hard stop?

      Show answer

      A hard stop placed with the order is a default; a mental stop is a hope that you'll act against your emotions in the moment.

    Takeaway Move decisions to calm moments and lock them in with rules, hard stops, checklists and friction; willpower loses in the heat of a loss.

    StateWarning signPre-decided response
    FearClosing winners early; can't click a valid setupReduce size until the risk feels small

    When trading becomes compulsive#

    • Fast, unpredictable rewards (intraday and F&O) light up the same loop as a slot machine: loss → urge to win it back → trade → brief relief → usually a bigger loss. Near-misses and the occasional big win keep it spinning.
    • Karan lost ₹50,000 he could spare, then took a personal loan and hid it from his wife. The worst part was that he couldn't stop.
    Red flag (last month or two)Red flag
    Borrowing, credit cards or bill money to trade or recoverTrading to escape stress, boredom, loneliness or low mood
    Hiding or lying about losses, to others or yourselfA strong pull to win it back immediately
    Deciding to stop, then trading anywaySleep, work or relationships suffering; restless when not in a trade
    A cluster of these is not a strategy problem, and a smaller position isn't the fix. Stop fully for weeks, tell someone you trust the real numbers, remove your own access (close or limit the account, hand over the login), and talk to a professional. The course points to India's free, 24×7 KIRAN mental-health helpline (1800-599-0019; check the current number) and problem-gambling support. Asking for help is a strength.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Name three red flags of compulsive trading.

      Show answer

      Any three of: borrowing or using bill money to trade, hiding losses, deciding to stop then trading anyway, trading to escape mood, a strong urge to win it back, sleep or relationships suffering.

    2. If several red flags fit, is trading smaller the fix?

      Show answer

      No. Stop fully for weeks, tell someone the real numbers, remove your own access and talk to a professional.

    Takeaway Borrowing to trade, hiding losses and failing to stop are signs of compulsion, not a strategy problem; stop fully and get help.

    02Hedging: when, why and when not

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Trading Psychology · module B, chapters 8–13 (what hedging does, when to hedge, when not to, cost, tools, hedge ratio)

    A hedge is insurance with a premium#

    A hedge canA hedge cannot
    Cap a known loss over a defined periodTurn a bad position into a good one
    Give a fixed, known worst caseProtect you forever for free
    Buy time and calm to decideGuarantee a profit
    Reduce one named riskRemove every risk at once
    • A hedge is a second position that pays when your main one hurts. It puts a floor under losses and usually lowers your ceiling: someone is taking your risk and wants paying.
    • The bill comes as a premium, as spreads and charges, or as upside given up (a hedge loses when your stocks rally).
    • Meera paid ≈ ₹4,000 for an index put on a ₹5 lakh portfolio before the Budget; it expired worthless. That's insurance working, not failing.
    • If a hedge looks like free money, the cost is hiding somewhere. If you want it to make money, it's a second bet, not a hedge.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Meera's ₹4,000 Budget put expired worthless. Did the hedge fail?

      Show answer

      No. That's insurance working: it protected her through the event and the premium was the price.

    2. Name the three ways a hedge charges you.

      Show answer

      A premium, spreads and charges, or upside given up.

    Takeaway A hedge caps a named loss for a period at a cost; if it looks free, the cost is hidden, and if you want it to profit, it's a second bet.

    When hedging earns its cost#

    SituationWhy a hedge fits
    Big portfolio into a known event (Budget, election result, RBI policy)Gaps skip stops; a hedge pays however far price jumps
    Concentrated stock you can't sell (locked ESOPs, tax on exit)The only lever left on one name's 15–20% overnight risk
    Overnight or weekend F&O into newsLeverage plus a gap you can't react to
    Real business exposure (importer, exporter, jeweller)Turns an unknown future price into a known one
    A known cash need (₹20 lakh in 3 months)Protects the amount on the date it's needed

    The test: real and sizable, time-bound, and hard to remove by selling. Meera's ₹45 lakh book faced a possible 5–7% Budget-day swing (a 6% fall ≈ ₹2.7 lakh), so a few thousand rupees of NIFTY put was a fair trade. Know what you protect, for how long, and when the hedge comes off.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why does a hedge help into a Budget or election result more than a stop does?

      Show answer

      Gaps skip stops; a hedge pays however far price jumps.

    2. What three tests make a risk worth hedging?

      Show answer

      It's real and sizable, time-bound, and hard to remove by selling.

    Takeaway Hedges earn their cost before known events, on concentrated holdings you can't sell, on leveraged overnight positions, and on real business exposure.

    When not to hedge#

    1. Small, survivable book: ₹80,000 dipping to ₹70,000 isn't worth ₹1,000–1,500 of premium a month.
    2. Long-term SIP: a fall is a discount; hedging cancels what makes a SIP work.
    3. Tiny position: make it smaller or close it.
    4. Plain fear: emotional hedging protects your nerves, not your capital, and bills you each time.
    5. Too expensive: pre-event puts can cost 3–4% of value against a feared 5% fall.
    6. Can't name the risk: vague fear buys vague, badly matched protection.

    Hedge costs are near-fixed, so they swallow a small book's return. When a position scares you, cutting it is usually cleaner: no premium, no expiry, no basis risk, capital freed. If it must be hedged to sleep, it was probably sized too big. Exceptions: unsurvivable downside, option sellers' tails, positions you genuinely can't exit.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why shouldn't a long-term SIP investor hedge a fall?

      Show answer

      A fall is a discount for a SIP; hedging cancels what makes the SIP work.

    2. What's usually cleaner than hedging a position that scares you?

      Show answer

      Cutting it: no premium, no expiry, no basis risk, and capital freed.

    Takeaway Don't hedge small books, SIPs, tiny positions or vague fear; if you must hedge to sleep, the position is probably too big.

    The full cost of protection#

    One month on ₹5 lakh (illustrative)₹
    Option premium (≈ 1.5%)7,500
    Bid-ask spread, entry and exit≈ 400
    Brokerage, exchange, GST≈ 150
    STT and statutory fees≈ 100
    Slippage≈ 200
    All-in≈ 8,350 (≈ 1.7%)
    • Hidden costs beyond the premium: time decay (the premium melts to zero if nothing happens), basis risk (an index put may barely move while your stocks fall), margin blocked on a futures hedge, and upside capped by collars and covered calls.
    • Rolled every month, ≈ 1.7% becomes ≈ 18–20% a year even in a calm year: Meera spent ≈ ₹90,000 protecting ₹5 lakh that was never in danger.
    • Premiums jump when fear is high: 1.5% in calm times can be 4–5% in a panic. Price the hedge for today's conditions.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. A hedge costs about 1.7% a month all-in. Rolled for a year, roughly what?

    Takeaway Count premium, spread, charges and slippage; a roughly 1.7% monthly hedge rolled all year costs close to a fifth of the portfolio.

    Simple hedging tools, cheapest first#

    ToolCostMain trade-off
    Trim / hold cashNoneGive up gains on the part sold
    DiversifyNoneNo help in a broad crash (everything fell together in Mar 2020)
    Protective putPremium, recurringBleeds if nothing happens; keeps upside

Long-term investors mostly trim and diversify; swing traders trim or buy a put; futures traders use the index short; option sellers use collars and defined-risk structures.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Which tool costs nothing but gives up gains on the part sold?

      Show answer

      Trimming, or holding cash.

    2. What does a collar trade off?

      Show answer

      A floor below in exchange for a cap above; the call sold pays for the put.

    Takeaway Climb the ladder only as far as needed: trim, diversify, protective put, covered call, collar, short index future.

    Credit spreads, iron condors and iron flies#

    StructureBuildProfit zone
    Bull put spreadSell a higher put, buy a lower oneIndex stays above the short put
    Bear call spreadSell a lower call, buy a higher oneIndex stays below the short call

    • In every one the credit is smaller than the capped loss: the price of winning often.
    • Meera's weekly condors made ≈ ₹4,000 a time; six wins (+₹24,000) were wiped out by one Budget-week trend (≈ −₹28,000). A 90% win rate can still lose money.

    • Failure modes: a fast move through both strikes; pin risk when the index settles at the short strike (cash-settled, so close before expiry); four legs of charges eating a small credit.

    Adjustment risk: managing or hiding?#

    • Moves: roll to a later expiry, shift strikes, add or remove legs, double down. Each can manage risk or disguise not booking a loss.
    • One test: does it reduce or define risk, or grow it to avoid a loss? Good adjustments are planned in advance, keep the max loss the same or smaller, are often done for a credit, and don't grow size.
    • Arjun's put spread (₹5,000 credit, ₹15,000 max loss) was rolled, its protective leg removed to fund the roll, then doubled. A gap-down cost ≈ ₹1.4 lakh. Each step grew the max loss: ₹15k → ₹30k → ₹60k → open-ended.
    • This is loss aversion in skill's language: "I'm rolling", "I'm early", "I'll average down". Rolling often works a few times, which teaches the wrong lesson.
    • Write both the planned adjustment and the "just close it" point before entering. Never adjust because booking the loss hurts.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What is the one test for any adjustment?

      Show answer

    2. Why is a roll that works a few times dangerous?

      Show answer

    Takeaway Good adjustments reduce or define risk; rolling, removing wings and doubling up to avoid booking a loss is loss aversion in disguise.

    Tail risk: the day that erases months#

    • A seller collects small, capped premiums often and takes a rare loss that isn't capped the same way: a fat left tail. Meera's weekly strangles banked ≈ ₹4 lakh on ₹6 lakh in ten months; a global-shock gap took a ₹40 put to ₹520 and wiped ≈ nine months out in a day.
    • Three forces arrive together: an overnight gap that jumps your stop, a volatility explosion that reprices every option, and liquidity vanishing just when you need to exit. Real examples: March 2020 (a lower circuit and a ≈ 13% one-day fall) and the June 2024 election result (≈ 6% intraday).
    Survival habitWhy
    Never sell nakedA long wing defines the worst case before entry
    Margin buffer (use ½–⅔)Volatility spikes raise margin at the worst moment
    Standing far-OTM tail hedgeBleeds a little most months, explodes on the black-swan day
    Size for the gap, not the average dayIf the defined max hit tomorrow, would the account survive?
    Respect the calmLong quiet stretches are when sellers size up and hedge less

    Defences reduce the tail, they don't delete it: hedges cost real income, "defined" can still be a big number, and an extreme gap can exceed the hedge. The goal is surviving to trade next month.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Name the three forces that hit option sellers together on a tail day.

      Show answer

      An overnight gap that jumps stops, a volatility explosion, and liquidity vanishing.

    2. What's the point of a standing far-OTM tail hedge?

      Show answer

      It bleeds a little most months but pays off hugely on the black-swan day.

    Takeaway Sellers earn small premiums often and face rare, huge losses; gaps, volatility spikes and vanishing liquidity arrive together.

    Long-term investor plan#

    1. Allocate: a written mix, e.g. 60% equity, 30% debt, 10% gold (more equity if young, more debt as a goal nears).
    2. Automate the SIP: same amount, same date (say ₹10,000 a month); never stop it in a fall.
    3. Rebalance: once a year, or when a slice drifts 5–10% off target.
    4. Emergency fund: 6–12 months of expenses in cash, FD or a liquid fund, outside the market. Money needed within 5 years stays out of equity.
    5. Crash rule, pre-decided: do nothing or keep buying; selling isn't on the menu.

    In March 2020 Arjun watched funds fall ≈ 40% in five weeks, sold near the bottom into an FD, and watched the next year's ≈ 70% rise from the sidelines; Meera's SIP kept buying. An investor's biggest risk is behaviour, not the market. The crash rule flips for traders: they cut losers fast. It assumes broad, diversified holdings, not one junk stock.

      Show answer

      Show answer

    Takeaway A written allocation, an automatic SIP, yearly rebalancing, an emergency fund and a pre-decided 'do nothing in a crash' rule.

    Stock (swing) trader plan#

    capital ₹2,00,000 · risk 1% = ₹2,000 a tradeentry 500, stop 480 → ₹20 a share → 2,000 ÷ 20 = 100 sharesposition ₹50,000 (a quarter of capital), loss if stopped ₹2,000daily limit: −3% (₹6,000) or 3 losers, whichever first
    1. Setup: only your one defined pattern; boredom and tips aren't setups.
    2. Stop: pre-placed at entry, never "watched manually".
    3. Size: 1% risk ÷ stop distance; the stop, not conviction, sets quantity.
    4. Daily limit: hit it, screen off.
    5. Gap rule: a stop at 480 can fill at 460 (₹4,000, double the plan). Size overnight holds for ≈ 1.5–2× the stop distance; no big size into results.
    6. Journal: setup, risk, result, rules followed; review every Sunday.

    Arjun sized by feel and had no stop; one results gap cost more than his last eleven wins.

    Takeaway Risk 1% at the stop, let stop distance set quantity, cap the day at −3% or three losers, and size overnight holds for gaps.

    Futures trader plan#

    1 NIFTY lot ≈ 24,000 × 65 = ₹15.6 lakh notional (≈ 9× on ≈ ₹1.8 lakh margin)1% index move = ₹15,600 · 350-point gap = ₹22,750
    1. Leverage cap: margin under about half of capital.
    2. MTM buffer: keep the other half as spare cash for daily losses and margin hikes.
    3. Per-trade risk: 1–2% (₹2,000–4,000 on ₹2 lakh), stop set before entry.
    4. Overnight: smaller size; flat before known events.
    5. Rollover: 2–3 days before expiry, never on expiry day.
    6. Daily stop: about −4% (₹8,000), then no revenge.

    Arjun used ₹1.8 lakh of ₹2 lakh as margin; a 350-point overnight gap exceeded his ₹20,000 cushion and he was squared off near the low. Survival is about not being forced out, not being right. Volatility raises margins too.

    Option buyer plan#

    1. Premium budget: 1–2% of capital per buy, ≈ 5% a month in total (₹10,000 on ₹2 lakh).
    2. Total-loss test: fine if it goes to zero, because it often will.
    3. Expiry with time: weeks out, not same-day or weekly lottery tickets.
    4. IV check: not pumped by a near event, or wait.
    5. Strike: close to the money, not a far-OTM jackpot.
    6. Exit decided now: out at −50% of premium, or by a set day.

    ₹50 × 65 = ₹3,250 fits 2% of ₹2 lakh; the −50% stop caps the loss near ₹1,625. ₹120 × 65 = ₹7,800 (3.9%) is too big. Vikram's weekly ₹4–7 calls (≈ ₹325 a lot) bled a third of his account in four months.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. ₹2 lakh capital: is a ₹120 premium on a 65-lot (₹7,800) within the plan?

      Show answer

      No. It's 3.9%, above the 1–2% per-buy budget.

    2. What exit rule caps an option buy's loss?

      Show answer

      Out at −50% of premium, or by a set day.

    Takeaway Spend 1–2% of capital per buy, accept total loss, buy time, avoid pumped IV, and decide the exit before entry.

    Option seller plan#

    1. Defined risk only: spreads, never naked; each position's max loss under ≈ 2% (₹10,000 on ₹5 lakh), and all at max together survivable (≈ 6–8%).
    2. Margin buffer: use at most 50–60% of margin.
    3. Standing tail hedge: cheap far-OTM longs always on (≈ 0.5–1% a month).
    4. Adjustment rule written before entry: one adjustment, roll or close, never add naked size.
    5. Max-loss day: −3% (≈ ₹15,000) open plus realised, then close everything.
    6. Event filter: no fresh selling into the Budget, policy, elections or big results.

    Arjun used spreads but had no tail hedge and no daily stop; three spreads hit max on one gap morning, he added size, and gave back four months by lunch. Selling forgives the least, so its plan is the strictest.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What's the option seller's max-loss day rule?

      Show answer

      At about −3% open plus realised, close everything.

    2. What should never be the response when spreads hit max loss together?

      Show answer

      Adding size.

    Takeaway Spreads only, a margin buffer, a standing tail hedge, one planned adjustment and a max-loss day: selling forgives least, so its plan is strictest.

    Hedger plan: five rows before any hedge#

    RowFill in
    ExposureWhich position, how much, against what risk, in one sentence ("I'm nervous" isn't an exposure)
    SizeFull or partial from value × beta ÷ lot notional; never bigger than the exposure. ₹50 lakh × 1.2 = ₹60 lakh ÷ ₹15.6 lakh ≈ 3.8 lots (≈ 4 full, 2 partial)
    CostAll-in: premium (e.g. ₹200 × 65 = ₹13,000 a lot) or margin, spread, charges, upside given up (≈ ₹45,000 ≈ 0.9% for two weeks on ₹50 lakh)
    ExpiryCovers the whole risk window; roll if the risk outlasts it
    Exit"Comes off when ___", plus a calendar reminder

    Arjun shorted NIFTY futures against ₹50 lakh before a Budget, forgot them, and earned nothing in an 8% rally: the hedge became a bet against his own book. Investors remove after the event or sale; F&O traders when the position closes; businesses when the cash flow settles. Basis risk and chunky lots remain.

      Show answer

      Show answer

    Takeaway Before any hedge, fill five rows: exposure, size, cost, expiry and exit; a forgotten hedge becomes a bet against your own book.
