    01Risk is not just losing money

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Risk Management · module A, chapters 1–4 (what risk means, survive first, risk vs reward, the risk pyramid)

    What risk really means#

    • Risk is anything that can knock you out of the game: lost capital, but also lost sleep, discipline, time, and being forced to sell at the worst price.
    • Temporary dip: a sound asset falls and can recover. Permanent loss: the business is broken, or a sound asset was sold at the bottom.
    • Arjun put ₹3 lakh into large companies in Jan 2020; NIFTY fell ≈ 38% by late March, his portfolio showed ≈ ₹1.85 lakh and he sold. The market made new highs within about a year: the sale, not the fall, made the loss permanent.
    • Three things create forced sellers: leverage (the broker squares you off), money needed soon, and emotional capacity running out.
    • Quiet, self-made risks matter most: widening a stop "just this once", 40% of savings in one weekly expiry, leverage taken on a calm day. Calm is not safe.
    Test: would a 35% fall trigger a margin call or break this month's budget? Then the position is too big.

    Survive first: the recovery math#

    gain needed to get back = loss ÷ (1 − loss)−10% → +11% · −25% → +33% · −50% → +100%−75% → +300% · −90% → +900%
    • Risk of ruin: the chance losses get so deep you can't continue. It rises fast with the share of the account risked per trade.
    • Risking about a third per trade: 3–4 losses in a row and you're done (0.67³ leaves ≈ 30%). Risking 1–2%: 10 straight losses leave ≈ 82–90% of the account.
    • COVID 2020: NIFTY fell ≈ 38–40% in weeks and needed ≈ 65% to recover, close to a year, and that was a diversified index.
    • Ravi grew ₹1 lakh to ≈ ₹1.3 lakh, then a gap-down on an oversized leveraged position left ₹41,000 by lunch. The mistake was size, not direction.
    • SEBI's Sept 2024 study: ≈ 91% of individual equity-derivatives traders lost money in FY24; uncontrolled size is a central cause. After losses, trade smaller, never bigger.

    Risk vs reward: name the risk#

    KindWhat it isResponse
    KnownVisible, measurable (a 38–40% index crash)Plan and size for it
    UnknownFraud, war, rule changes, pandemicsNever bet so big one surprise ends you
    IgnoredSeen but denied (moving a stop, holding a mistake)Stop pretending: it's avoidable
    • You're paid only for carrying risk. FD ≈ 7%: low risk, low reward. A small-cap multibagger can also fall 70–80% or become impossible to exit.
    • "Guaranteed 3% a month" compounds to ≈ 43% a year: a high return with "no risk" is a lie, often paid to early joiners from later money.
    1. What's the reward in rupees, and when?
    2. Where exactly is the risk? If nobody can say, that's the answer.
    3. What's the worst realistic loss, and could you survive it?
    4. Why is this being offered to me?

    The risk pyramid#

    LayerWhat goes there
    Base (outside the market)Emergency fund 3–6 months, health and term insurance, no high-interest debt
    InvestingGoals 5+ years out: index funds, quality businesses. The bulk.
    TradingDays to weeks; planned exit and stop on every position. Thinner.
    Speculation (tip)Weekly F&O, hot small caps: only money you'd never miss
    • Teaching split per ₹100 after the base: ₹80 investing, ₹15 trading, ₹5 speculation. Many beginners keep the top two at zero while learning.
    • Never in the market: rent, this month's EMI, fees due in 1–3 years, borrowed or margin-funded money.
    • Meera, 26, put a ₹1.2 lakh bonus into expiry-day BANKNIFTY options; by 3 pm it was ≈ ₹18,000, and ₹40,000 of it was her father's surgery deposit. The error was letting money that wasn't hers to risk enter the market.

    02The money-safety system

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Risk Management · module B, chapters 5–8 (fix your base, capital buckets, time horizon, capacity vs tolerance)

    Fix your base: five pillars#

    PillarRule of thumb
    Emergency fund≈ 6 months of expenses in cash or a liquid fund, never in stocks (₹40,000 × 6 = ₹2.4 lakh)
    Health insuranceYour own policy for you and family, not only the employer's
    Term lifeOnly if others depend on your income: pure cover, no investment mixed in
    No costly debtCards and personal loans charge 14–40%; clearing 18% is a sure, tax-free 18%
    Income stabilitySalaried ≈ 6 months; freelancers and business owners 9–12 months

    Arjun had ₹4 lakh invested and no buffer; he lost his job in early 2020 and sold near the March lows to pay rent. The market roughly doubled off the lows within a year. The base turns a life shock into a personal problem instead of a forced sale.

    Capital buckets and the walls between them#

    BucketHorizon and jobSize
    Long-term investing5+ years: index funds, SIPs, quality stocksLargest
    Short-term goal1–3 years: car, wedding, deposit. Kept whole.As needed
    TradingDays to weeks, always with a stop and a planSmall, capped
    F&O / speculationAssume total loss is possibleTiny
    LearningTuition while you learn (a few thousand rupees)Smallest
    • The rule: a loss in one bucket must never drain another. Keep goal money in an FD or liquid fund you'd have to break consciously.
    • An empty risk bucket means the system worked: stop. Refill only from genuinely spare money.
    • Arjun ran salary, savings and weekly options through one account; one bad expiry cost ≈ ₹35,000, his sister's college fee due in two months.

    Time horizon picks the asset#

    Needed inWhere it sits
    Under 1 yearSavings account or liquid fund
    1–3 yearsMostly debt funds and short FDs
    3–7 yearsBalanced debt and equity
    7+ yearsMostly equity (index funds, quality stocks)
    • Short money: equities can fall 30–50% and stay down for years; when the need date and the market clash, you sell low.
    • Long money: inflation is the threat. Prices up ≈ 6% a year against 3–4% in savings is a guaranteed slow loss.
    • Arjun moved a ₹8 lakh down payment into stocks in late 2019; it was ≈ ₹5.2 lakh in March 2020 when the builder's payment fell due. The mismatch was the mistake.
    • Shorter deadlines, like an F&O expiry, need tighter risk control, not looser: you can't wait for recovery.

    Capacity vs tolerance#

    capacity = what your finances can survive (arithmetic)tolerance = what your nerves can hold through a fallusable risk = the LOWER of the two
    ProfileCapacity / toleranceSensible level
    Young salariedHigh / untestedModerate; build the habit
    RetireeLow / often calmLow: capacity caps it
    Lumpy-income business ownerUnstable / variesLow–moderate, bigger base
    Full-time traderFragile / highModerate, strict per-trade caps
    1. Keep the emergency fund full; score capacity (income, dependents, debt, horizon).
    2. Score tolerance with the 2 am test: at −20%, hold, freeze or sell?
    3. Take the lower score and turn it into rupees: ₹2 lakh × 20% = ₹40,000 of pre-accepted pain.
    4. Write it down; re-check yearly and after life changes. Tolerance is overstated in bull markets.

    03Investor risk management

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Risk Management · module C, chapters 9–14 (asset allocation, diversification, SIPs and lumpsum, rebalancing, concentrated stock risk, investor mistakes)

    Asset allocation: the biggest decision#

    • The split across equity, debt, gold and cash sets how violent the ride is; stock picking only decides how you do against the market. For investors, allocation is the main risk lever.
    • A 40% market fall costs a 70%-equity portfolio ≈ 28%, a 30%-equity one ≈ 12%.
    • Jan–Mar 2020 (illustrative): NIFTY ≈ −38%, gold in rupees ≈ +8%, debt ≈ flat. A 60/30/10 mix lost ≈ 22% and needed ≈ +28% to recover, against ≈ +61% for pure equity.
    StageEquityDebtGold / cash
    20s70–80%10–20%5–10%
    30s–40s55–70%20–35%5–10%
    50s40–55%35–50%5–10%
    60s+25–40%50–65%5–15%

    Shortcut: equity ≈ 100 − age. A pension supports more equity; panicking at a 10% dip argues for less. All-equity invites panic selling; all-FD or gold barely beats inflation after tax. In a real panic everything can dip briefly, gold included.

    Diversification without overdoing it#

    • It removes company risk (fraud, debt, a lost client), never market risk; in a crash correlations jump towards 1 and most stocks fall together.
    • Most of the benefit arrives by ≈ 15–20 stocks; past 20–30 names you add fees and confusion, not safety ("diworsification"). Forty overlapping funds buy index returns at higher cost.
    • A ₹5,000-a-month SIP into a NIFTY index fund owns 50 large companies at once.
    • Meera put ₹5 lakh into one stock in 2018 and lost most of it when the story cracked.
    1. No single stock above ≈ 5–10% of the portfolio.
    2. At least 4–5 sectors: ten bank stocks are one bet.
    3. Some non-equity assets: debt or FDs, maybe gold.
    4. Every holding nameable and tracked; funds that don't secretly hold the same large caps.

    • 753.97 units for ₹60,000: average cost ≈ ₹79.58. At ₹100 that's ≈ ₹75,397, a gain while the price ended where it began. A ₹60,000 lumpsum at ₹100 ends flat.
    • A SIP's real value is behavioural: it keeps buying through fear. In a straight rising market a lumpsum wins because money works sooner.
    • Lumpsum suits money already in hand, a long horizon and the stomach for an early fall. Stopping a SIP in a crash cancels the cheapest buying, as Ravi did in 2020 while Meera's ₹10,000 SIP kept going.
    • Averaging fails on a single stock heading to zero, or when you must sell mid-fall.

    Rebalancing: sell winners, buy laggards#

    start ₹10 lakh = ₹6 lakh equity (60%) + ₹4 lakh debt (40%)equity doubles → ₹12 lakh + ₹4 lakh = ₹16 lakh (75 / 25)target 60% of ₹16 lakh = ₹9.6 lakh equity, ₹6.4 lakh debtaction sell ₹2.4 lakh of equity, move it to debt
    MethodHow
    CalendarReset on a fixed date, e.g. yearly
    BandAct when a sleeve drifts 5–10 points (60% target → 50–70% band)
    HybridCheck on the date, trade only if a band is breached

    Drift is a hidden risk increase, not a win. Once a year is enough for beginners: more often adds capital-gains tax, exit loads and charges. Route fresh SIP money to the laggard to rebalance without selling. Rebalance only between sound, diversified sleeves, never into a collapsing stock.

    Concentrated stock risk#

    • An employee holding employer stock carries one bet three ways: salary, vested ESOPs or RSUs, and shares bought. A bad patch can cut all three at once.
    • Concentration builds fortunes and destroys them from the same starting decision; trimming keeps upside while limiting ruin.
    • Traps: employer loyalty, family business plus same-sector savings, a favourite stock, IPO hype. A healthy 10% position left alone can drift to a reckless 60%.
    1. Add up true exposure (salary, vested and unvested ESOPs, shares, fund weight) as a share of net worth.
    2. Set a ceiling while calm: illustratively 10–20% of investable wealth in one stock.
    3. Trim on a schedule (a set fraction each quarter), using vesting dates and strong rallies.
    4. Plan tax timing, possibly across financial years; redeploy into broad index funds or other sectors.
    5. Keep 3–6 months of expenses outside the employer's stock.

    Investor mistakes that look smart#

    MistakeBetter move
    Averaging down with no thesisA written reason the business is sound, a fixed total size and pre-planned tranches
    Chasing last year's winnerStick to the plan; diversify broadly
    Panic-selling a crashOwn only what you can hold through a 30–40% fall; keep the emergency fund
    "It fell, so it's cheap"Judge the business, not the old high: −70% can be followed by another −70%
    Trusting a fat dividend yieldYield = dividend ÷ price, so it spikes as the price collapses; check why

    Meera bought at ₹500 and averaged down at ₹400, ₹300 and ₹200 without asking why it fell: a ₹50,000 mistake became ₹2 lakh. Each trap feels rational in the moment; ask for the actual reason before every add, sale or buy.

    Trading is not investing#

    InvestorTrader
    OwnsA slice of a businessA bet on a price move
    Works for youTime, growth, compoundingA repeatable edge
    HoldingYearsMinutes to days
    Exit decidedLooselyExactly, before entry
    Biggest riskWrong businessSkipping the risk rules
    • Every trade needs five tools: a pre-defined exit, a position size, a stop-loss, a journal entry and a daily risk limit.
    • Arjun bought at ₹1,020 because it "looked strong", held through ₹1,005 and ₹990 with no plan, and was down ₹18,000 by 3:15 pm: investor habits on a trade.
    • Edge is a small advantage that pays across many trades, not conviction in one. Relabelling a stuck trade as an investment isn't investing.

    • A wider stop means a smaller position for the same loss. Ravi bought 1,000 shares at ₹100 on a ₹2 lakh account by feel; a fall to ₹85 cost ₹15,000, over 7%.
    • Futures come in whole lots: a NIFTY lot of 65 (check current specs) with a 30-point stop risks ₹1,950. Option sellers face a tail far beyond the premium: size tiny, often one lot or none.
    • Percentage sizing shrinks after losses and grows after gains; fixed-rupee sizing doesn't adjust. Sizing caps the planned loss, not the worst case: a gap from a ₹92 stop to ₹80 doubles the hit to ≈ ₹5,000.

    Stop-loss: five kinds#

    TypeWhere it sitsUse when
    TechnicalJust beyond support, a swing low or a trendlineThe idea depends on a level
    Volatility1.5–2 × ATR from entryChoppy instruments
    TimeExit after X bars or days without progressIt should have moved by now
    MentalIn your head onlyAlmost never
    DisasterThe maximum tolerable loss, as a resting orderAlways, every style
    • Place a real order at entry and move it only in your favour. Arjun's ₹95 "plan" drifted to ₹92, ₹90 and lower; he sold at ₹81.
    • Avoid round numbers (₹500, NIFTY 24,000), where stops crowd, and stops inside normal noise: a ₹3 stop on a stock that swings ₹8 a day is a donation.
    • Stops fill at the next traded price, so a ₹95 stop on a stock opening at ₹80 fills near ₹80. The defence is size. Stop-outs before the move you expected are the insurance premium.

Typical active traders win 40–55%; option buyers win rarely but big; option sellers win often but face the fat loss.

    • Arjun lost ₹1,800 on two trades, sized up to win it back, took 9 trades instead of 3 and was ≈ −22% by lunch. A −3% daily stop would have ended the same morning flat.
    • F&O example: −3% a day, −8 to −10% a month. Option sellers: exit a short at 2–3 × the premium collected.
    • Limits that are too tight for the strategy's normal swing just cause noise stop-outs. The biggest failure is overriding one "just this once".

    Portfolio heat: total open risk#

    position risk = stop distance × quantityheat = Σ position risk across every open trade (as % of capital)gross = longs + shorts · net = longs − shorts
    • Priya (₹2 lakh) ran eight 1% trades, all financials: 8% heat. One bad sector day hit every stop for ₹16,000. Count themes, not tickers: correlated names behave like one larger bet, and correlations rise towards 1 in stress.
    • Long ₹60,000 and short ₹20,000: gross ₹80,000 in play, net ₹40,000 long.
    • Example caps: active traders ≈ 5–6% total open heat; F&O ≈ 3–5%; long-term investors no sector above ≈ 20–25%; option sellers cap the combined max loss and stress-test a gap.
    • Lot sizes cited for the Apr–Jun 2026 cycle: NIFTY 65, BANKNIFTY 30; exchanges revise them.

    05Leverage, margin and execution risk

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Risk Management · module E, chapters 22–26 (leverage, margin calls, the broker's RMS, slippage, liquidity and spread, gap and event risk)

    Leverage: measure moves against your margin#

    position = margin × leveragehit to your money = price move % × leverage2% move at 5× = 10% of your capital
    LeverageAdverse move that eats the whole margin
    1×Effectively never (the stock itself can still go to zero)
    2×50%
    5×20%
    10×10%
    20×5%
    • Vikram: ₹50,000 account, ₹2.5 lakh position (5×); an ordinary 4% morning dip cost ₹10,000, 20% of the account.
    • India: intraday equity ≈ 5× at most under peak-margin rules; futures carry built-in leverage (the course gives ≈ 5–6× in one chapter, ≈ 8× in another, depending on margin); option buying is leveraged through premium with loss capped; option selling posts margin with losses that can far exceed the premium.
    • Higher leverage shortens the time you have to be right: a normal dip forces you out before the move comes. The maximum offered is a ceiling, not a recommendation.
    • Work backwards: pick the worst move you must survive (say 15%) and choose leverage that move can't wipe out, often 1–2×. A futures lot's leverage can only be cut by trading fewer lots.

    Margin calls and forced exits#

    TermMeaning
    MarginA security deposit behind a leveraged position, not its price
    Mark-to-marketThe position revalued at the latest price; losses come out of margin
    ShortfallRemaining margin below what the position needs
    Square-offThe broker closes the position for you, without asking
    Free marginThe buffer that absorbs normal swings
    • Forced exits fire when price has moved hardest against you, so they land later and lower than your own stop; the difference is loss you never chose.
    • Available margin is a ceiling, not capital. A trader with ₹1 lakh saw ≈ ₹5 lakh "available", used nearly all of it on one index future and was squared off near the intraday low.
    • Peak-margin rules collect margin upfront and check it at random intraday snapshots; unclosed intraday positions are squared off mid-afternoon at whatever price prevails, and a sharp move can trigger it earlier.
    1. Size from your risk rule, then confirm margin stays comfortably free.
    2. Know how far price can move before a call; keep your stop well before that point.
    3. Add funds or cut early; exit intraday trades before the square-off window.

    The broker's risk system (RMS)#

    • An automatic margin guard that runs on the broker's rules and clock, protects the broker first, and closes positions at market on a shortfall without waiting for your stop.
    • Cash vs collateral: F&O needs a minimum cash component (roughly half, as the course states for 2026) because daily losses settle in cash, which pledged shares can't pay. All-collateral positions trigger cash shortfalls and penalties even when total margin looks full.
    • Haircut: pledging ₹1 lakh of stock typically gives ≈ ₹80,000–90,000 of margin; volatile or illiquid stocks get bigger haircuts, never zero.
    • Auto square-off: intraday (MIS) positions close in a window around 3:20–3:30 pm, broker and segment dependent. Exit before it yourself.
    • Peak-margin snapshots: a shortfall for a few minutes at a random snapshot can bring a penalty without any square-off. Aim to be covered at every snapshot.
    • Arjun ran pledged-only intraday futures, lost track of time and was closed at ≈ 3:25 pm below his stop in a thin market, then drew a peak-margin penalty on another day.

    Impact costAverage-fill drift as a big order walks up the book
    SlippageExpected price − actual fill
    • Meera's far-OTM option showed ₹12; a market order for a few lots walked ₹13.0 → ₹13.5 → ₹14.0 and averaged ≈ ₹13.40, ≈ 11% lost before the trade moved, with a similar slice due on exit.
    • Market orders guarantee the fill, not the price; limit orders guarantee the price, not the fill. Default to limits at or near the touch; use market only when urgent and the instrument is liquid.
    • Index futures and large caps trade a few paise wide; far-OTM options and small caps are thin, and your own order moves the price.
    • A ₹2 scalp with ₹1.50 of round-trip slippage keeps ₹0.50 even when right, before charges; the same ₹1.50 barely matters on a ₹40 swing. Thin edges get eaten first.

    Gap and event risk#

    Scheduled (known date, unknown outcome)Unscheduled (no warning)
    Company results; RBI and US Fed decisions; the Union Budget (1 Feb); weekly and monthly expiry; election counting (4 Jun 2024, a sharp NIFTY fall that morning)Demonetisation (Nov 2016, after the close); COVID (Mar 2020, gap-downs and lower-circuit halts); wars; an overnight foreign bank collapse; sudden regulatory orders
    • A stop caps the price you trigger at, not the price you fill at. Bought at ₹105 with a ₹100 stop; poor results; opened at ₹85: planned risk ₹5 a share, actual ₹20.
    • True overnight risk is the size of the surprise. On ₹1 lakh, a 15% gap costs ₹15,000 on a ₹1 lakh position and ₹7,500 on half that: you choose only the size.
    • Before an event ask: can I absorb a 10% adverse open in rupees? Is there leverage I can close before the bell? Am I short an option with open-ended loss? Protective options cap the loss but bleed premium; going to cash avoids gaps but misses moves.

    Why F&O is a different risk world#

    • Arjun bought 5 lots of the NIFTY 24,500 call at ₹40 (325 units, ₹13,000). NIFTY rallied to ≈ 24,300 and expired at 24,350, below the strike: right direction, total loss.
    • "It's only ₹40" isn't low risk. SEBI's Sept 2024 study: ≈ 91% of individual F&O traders lost in FY24, the average loser ≈ ₹1.2 lakh. Approach with humility and tiny size.

    • Monthly contracts must be rolled (spread, brokerage, slippage each time); basis (future − spot) converges to zero at expiry. Exchanges raise margins in volatile markets, often just as MTM losses arrive.
    • Don't "hold and wait" a future like a stock: fund a bad week in cash, or don't carry it.

    Option buyer: capped loss, low odds#

    • Max loss = premium; no margin calls. Call break-even = strike + premium, reached before expiry.
    • Killer 1, time decay: time value leaks daily, steepest in the last 2–3 days; cheap OTM weeklies are almost all time value.
    • Killer 2, timing: the move comes after expiry, or is too small or slow.
    • Killer 3, IV crush: events inflate premiums, then implied volatility collapses: ₹90 before results, ₹55 after, though the price rose a little.
    • Arjun bought far-OTM weekly calls at ₹6 most Tuesdays, NSE weekly expiry (₹390 a lot): 4 wins, 9 losses in three months, a slow bleed.
    1. Know the rupee premium at risk and accept losing all of it.
    2. Prefer near-the-money strikes with real value and more than 1–2 days left.
    3. Avoid IV inflated by an event; set a price or time exit before buying.

    • The equity curve is a gentle staircase, then a cliff. A volatility spike alone can reprice an ₹80 option to ₹500.
    • Index options are cash-settled; stock options and futures can go to physical delivery. If you don't understand settlement, close before expiry.
    • Beginner default: defined risk. Sell one option and buy a cheaper, further-out one (a spread), and keep size constant through winning streaks. Never sell naked.

    Greeks as a risk dashboard#

    GaugeMeasuresWho it hurts
    DeltaPrice change per 1-point move (≈ 0.5 → ≈ 50 paise)Anyone on the wrong side
    ThetaDaily time decay, weekends includedBuyers, every day (sellers' income)
    VegaSensitivity to implied volatilityBuyers after IV crush; sellers on IV spikes
    GammaHow fast delta changes; explosive near the strike at expiryHolders near the strike on expiry day

    Arjun bought a NIFTY call at ₹120 before an RBI policy; NIFTY rose ≈ 80 points by the next morning and the call was worth ₹95, sunk by theta and vega. Read all four gauges, not just delta. Greeks describe small, smooth moves (gaps break them) and say which risks you carry, not how much: sizing is separate.

    Expiry-day risk#

    • Weekly index options settle on Tuesday at NSE and Thursday at BSE (as the course states; exchanges revise these).
    • Gamma peaks near the strike, so a 20–30 point wiggle can swing the premium within minutes.
    • Liquidity trap: a ₹2 screen price may sell for ₹0.50, or find no bid. Far-OTM expiry options at 50 paise to ₹1 should be assumed worthless.
    • Pin risk: the index sits on a heavy strike, then breaks away. Execution freeze: queues lengthen and fills land far from the screen.
    • Adjustment panic: adding to, hedging or rolling a losing expiry trade usually deepens it.
    • Arjun put ₹8,000 into a ₹4 call hoping for a ten-bagger; NIFTY rose ≈ 30 points, the call touched ₹5, was 30 paise at 3:20 pm and zero at the close.
    Before an expiry trade: is it cheap because it's unlikely? Is there a real bid to exit into? Is the loss limit set, and will you take it without adjusting?
