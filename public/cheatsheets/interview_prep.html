    How to use this sheet#

    1. Start with the SDE II bar so you know what "good" looks like in each round.
    2. Build your story bank early: eight stories cover almost every behavioural question.
    3. Drill SQL patterns by writing them, not reading them; window functions come up most.
    4. Skim the fundamentals the week before; they show up as follow-ups inside system design.
    Pairs with the DSA, ML and system design sheets. Company names and levels vary; the bar described here is a common industry pattern, not any one company's rubric.

    01The SDE II bar: what each round is scoring

    common published rubrics and interviewer guides (Google, Amazon, Meta engineering levels; Gergely Orosz, The Software Engineer's Guidebook); levels and wording vary by company.

    SDE II expectations by round#

    RoundMeets the barAbove the barBelow the bar
    CodingOptimal approach largely unprompted; clean, runnable code for a medium in ~25 min; tests own codeHandles follow-ups (streaming, scale, a new constraint) and explains trade-offs crisplyNeeds heavy hints, silent coding, untested or non-running code
    System designClarifies scope, estimates, clean end-to-end design, two solid deep dives, names failure modesDrives the conversation, quantifies trade-offs, raises operational concerns unpromptedJumps to boxes, no numbers, can't go deep on any component
    Low-level designClear entities and responsibilities, runnable core, extensible via interfacesConcurrency and edge cases handled, tests sketchedGod classes, patterns for their own sake, no working code
    ML depth (ML engineer)Picks sensible baselines and metrics, explains models and failure modes, knows serving and monitoringConnects offline metrics to business impact; production stories with numbersModel-zoo answers with no data, metric or deployment thinking
    BehaviouralOwned a feature or service end to end; handled conflict and ambiguity; measurable resultsLed cross-team work, mentored others, changed a team's process"We" stories with no personal role, no outcome, blames others

    SDE I executes well-defined tasks; SDE II owns a well-defined area end to end with little supervision; senior engineers set direction across teams. Interviewers look for evidence of that scope in every round.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What does meeting the bar look like in an SDE II coding round?

      Show answer

      Reaching the optimal approach largely unprompted, clean runnable code for a medium in about 25 minutes, and testing it yourself.

    2. What separates SDE I, SDE II and senior?

      Show answer

      SDE I executes defined tasks, SDE II owns a defined area end to end, seniors set direction across teams.

    Takeaway SDE II owns a defined area end to end with little supervision; every round looks for evidence of that scope.

    Signals interviewers write down#

    • Problem solving: asks clarifying questions, considers alternatives, explains why the chosen approach wins.
    • Communication: thinks aloud, checks in at decision points, summarises before coding.
    • Code quality: readable names, small functions, handles edge cases, correct complexity.
    • Verification: dry-runs, picks tests that could break the code, fixes bugs calmly.
    • Ownership and scope: stories show you deciding, not just doing what you were told.
    • Write-ups are evidence-based: say the thing you want written down ("I'd pick a heap because…").
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Name the five signal areas interviewers record.

      Show answer

      Problem solving, communication, code quality, verification, and ownership and scope.

    2. Why say your reasoning aloud, like 'I'd pick a heap because…'?

      Show answer

      Write-ups are evidence-based; unsaid reasoning can't be credited.

    Takeaway Problem solving, communication, code quality, verification and ownership; write-ups are evidence-based, so say what you want written down.

    02Behavioural rounds: STAR and the story bank

    Amazon Leadership Principles interview guidance; "STAR method" guides from university career services; Gayle Laakmann McDowell, Cracking the Coding Interview (behavioural chapter).

    STAR, with timing#

    PartShare of answerWhat to say
    Situation≈ 10%Team, product, scale, why it mattered (two sentences)
    Task≈ 10%Your goal or responsibility, and the constraint (deadline, risk)
    Action≈ 60%What you did, step by step, including the decisions and trade-offs
    Result≈ 20%Numbers (latency, cost, revenue, incidents), what you learned, what changed afterwards

    Aim for two to three minutes, then stop and let them dig. Say "I", not "we", for your part. Have a follow-up ready: what would you do differently?

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Which STAR part should take most of the answer?

      Show answer

      Action, about 60%: what you did, with decisions and trade-offs.

    2. How long should a STAR answer run before you stop?

      Show answer

      About two to three minutes, then let them dig.

    Takeaway Situation and task about 20%, action about 60%, result about 20%; two to three minutes, 'I' not 'we'.

    Story themeAnswers questions about

    Write each story once in STAR with real numbers, then tag it with every theme it can answer. Most loops ask five to eight behavioural questions in total.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Name four of the eight story themes.

      Show answer

      Any four of: hardest technical problem, owned end to end, conflict, failure, ambiguity, tight deadline, mentoring, improving a process.

    2. How many behavioural questions do loops usually ask in total?

      Show answer

      About five to eight.

    Takeaway Eight real stories with numbers, each tagged with every theme it can answer, cover most behavioural questions.

    A worked STAR story (ML engineer)#

    PartExample
    SituationOur fraud model's features were computed twice, in a nightly Spark job for training and in Python for online scoring; precision had dropped from 0.82 to 0.71 over a quarter.
    TaskI was asked to find the cause and stop it recurring, without pausing the model, within one sprint.
    ActionI sampled 5,000 transactions and compared online and offline feature values; three features differed, one by a time-zone bug. I proposed moving the feature definitions into a shared feature store, wrote the parity test, migrated the top 20 features first, and added a CI check that fails if online and offline values diverge by more than 1%.
    ResultPrecision recovered to 0.81 within two weeks, at the same recall; the parity check caught two more mismatches in the next quarter before release. The team adopted the shared definitions for all new features.

    Why it works: a measurable problem, a clear personal role, decisions explained, numbers in the result, and a lasting change. Swap in your own story; never invent one.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. In the worked example, what caused the precision drop?

      Show answer

      Training/serving skew: features computed differently offline and online, including a time-zone bug.

    2. What lasting change made the result stick?

      Show answer

      A shared feature store and a CI parity check that fails if online and offline values diverge.

    Takeaway A good story has a measurable problem, a clear personal role, explained decisions, numbers in the result and a lasting change.

    Behavioural traps#

    • "We" all the way through: the interviewer can't credit you. Separate your actions from the team's.
    • No result or no numbers: estimate if exact figures are confidential ("about a third faster").
    • Blaming others in conflict stories: show how you understood their view and what you did.
    • A fake weakness ("I work too hard"): pick a real, minor one and what you are doing about it.
    • Rambling: long situation, short action. Flip it.
    • One story for everything: reuse is fine twice, not five times.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What if exact result figures are confidential?

      Show answer

      Estimate, e.g. 'about a third faster'.

    2. How should you fix a rambling answer?

      Show answer

      Shorten the situation and give the action most of the time.

    Takeaway Avoid 'we' stories, missing numbers, blaming others, fake weaknesses, rambling set-ups and one story for everything.

    03SQL for interviews

    PostgreSQL documentation (queries, window functions, EXPLAIN); Markus Winand, Use the Index, Luke; common SQL interview question banks.

    Logical execution order#

    FROM / JOIN → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → window functions→ SELECT → DISTINCT → ORDER BY → LIMIT / OFFSET
    • That's why a SELECT alias can't be used in WHERE but can be used in ORDER BY.
    • WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after aggregation.
    • Window functions run after grouping, so you can't filter on them in WHERE: wrap the query in a CTE or subquery.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why can't you use a SELECT alias in WHERE?

      Show answer

      WHERE runs before SELECT in logical order; ORDER BY runs after, so aliases work there.

    2. How do you filter on a window function result?

      Show answer

      Wrap the query in a CTE or subquery and filter outside, since windows run after WHERE.

    Takeaway FROM/JOIN → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → windows → SELECT → DISTINCT → ORDER BY → LIMIT.

    Joins at a glance#

    JoinReturnsTypical use
    INNEROnly matching rows on both sidesOrders with their customers
    LEFTAll left rows, NULLs where no matchCustomers including those with no orders
    FULL OUTERAll rows from both, NULLs where unmatchedReconciling two sources
    CROSSEvery combinationCalendar × product grids
    SELFA table joined to itselfEmployee → manager, consecutive events
    Anti-joinLEFT JOIN … WHERE b.id IS NULL or NOT EXISTSUsers who never purchased

    Watch row multiplication: joining to a one-to-many table before aggregating double counts. Aggregate first, then join.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. How do you find users who never purchased?

      Show answer

      An anti-join: LEFT JOIN … WHERE right.id IS NULL, or NOT EXISTS.

    2. Why aggregate before joining a one-to-many table?

      Show answer

      Joining first multiplies rows and double counts.

    Takeaway Inner keeps matches, left keeps all left rows, full keeps both, cross multiplies, anti-join finds non-matches; aggregate before joining one-to-many.

    Window functions#

    FunctionDoesExample
    ROW_NUMBER()Unique sequence per partitionLatest order per user: ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY user_id ORDER BY created_at DESC) = 1
    RANK() / DENSE_RANK()Ties share a rank; RANK leaves gaps, DENSE_RANK doesn'tTop 3 salaries per department
    LAG() / LEAD()Previous or next row's valueDay-over-day change, time between sessions
    SUM() OVER (ORDER BY …)Running totalCumulative revenue
    AVG() OVER (… ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)Moving average7-day rolling average
    NTILE(n)Splits into n bucketsCustomer deciles by spend

    A window keeps every row (unlike GROUP BY). PARTITION BY restarts the calculation per group; ORDER BY inside OVER defines the running order and default frame.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. RANK vs DENSE_RANK?

      Show answer

      Both give ties the same rank; RANK leaves gaps after ties, DENSE_RANK doesn't.

    2. Which function gives day-over-day change?

      Show answer

      LAG (or LEAD) to read the previous or next row.

    Takeaway Windows keep every row; PARTITION BY restarts per group and ORDER BY inside OVER sets the running order.

    Classic query patterns#

    AskPattern
    Nth highest salaryDENSE_RANK() in a CTE, filter rank = N
    Top N per groupROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY g ORDER BY x DESC) <= N
    Remove duplicatesKeep ROW_NUMBER() = 1 per duplicate key
    Consecutive days (gaps and islands)date − ROW_NUMBER() is constant within a streak; group by it
    Month-over-month growthAggregate by month, then LAG()
    PivotSUM(CASE WHEN status='x' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) per column
    Retention or funnelsCohort by first event, then count later events by period
    MedianPERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY x)
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. How do you solve gaps and islands (consecutive days)?

      Show answer

      date − ROW_NUMBER() is constant within a streak; group by that difference.

    2. How do you remove duplicates?

      Show answer

      Keep ROW_NUMBER() = 1 per duplicate key.

    Takeaway DENSE_RANK for Nth highest, ROW_NUMBER for top-N and dedup, date minus ROW_NUMBER for streaks, LAG for growth, SUM(CASE) to pivot.

    NULL and aggregation traps#

    • NULL = NULL is unknown, not true: use IS NULL / IS DISTINCT FROM.
    • COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(col) skips NULLs; AVG ignores NULLs (it is not "treat as zero").
    • NOT IN (subquery) returns nothing if the subquery has a NULL; prefer NOT EXISTS.
    • A LEFT JOIN followed by a WHERE on the right table silently becomes an inner join; put the condition in ON.
    • Integer division: 1/2 = 0 in many engines; cast before dividing.
    • Every non-aggregated column in SELECT must be in GROUP BY (strict engines).
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why prefer NOT EXISTS over NOT IN (subquery)?

      Show answer

      If the subquery contains a NULL, NOT IN returns no rows.

    2. COUNT(*) vs COUNT(col)?

      Show answer

      COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(col) skips NULLs.

    Takeaway NULL = NULL is unknown; NOT IN with a NULL returns nothing; WHERE on the right table of a LEFT JOIN makes it inner.

    Indexes and EXPLAIN#

    • A B-tree index helps equality, range and ORDER BY on its leading columns. A composite index on (a, b) serves filters on a or a and b, not on b alone.
    • Indexes are skipped when you wrap the column in a function (WHERE LOWER(email) = …) unless you index the expression, or when the filter matches most rows.
    • A covering index includes every column the query needs, so the table isn't touched (index-only scan).
    • EXPLAIN ANALYZE shows the plan and real timings: look for sequential scans on big tables, misestimated row counts and nested loops over large inputs.
    • Each index speeds reads and slows writes; index for your top queries, not every column.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Does an index on (a, b) help a filter on b alone?

      Show answer

      No; it serves a, or a and b, not b alone.

    2. What do you look for in EXPLAIN ANALYZE?

      Show answer

      Sequential scans on big tables, misestimated row counts and nested loops over large inputs.

    Takeaway B-trees serve leading columns; functions on the column skip the index; covering indexes avoid the table; each index slows writes.

    04Operating systems quick reference

    Arpaci-Dusseau, Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces; Silberschatz, Operating System Concepts; Linux man pages.

    Processes vs threads#

    ProcessThread
    MemoryOwn address spaceShares the process's address space
    Creation and switch costHigher (new page tables)Lower
    IsolationA crash stays containedA bad thread can corrupt the whole process
    CommunicationIPC: pipes, sockets, shared memoryShared variables, guarded by locks
    • A context switch saves registers and the program counter, and for processes swaps the address space, flushing much of the TLB.
    • Python's GIL lets only one thread run Python bytecode at a time: use processes (or native code) for CPU-bound work, threads or asyncio for I/O-bound work.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why is a process switch costlier than a thread switch?

      Show answer

      It also swaps the address space, flushing much of the TLB.

    2. Why use processes for CPU-heavy Python?

      Show answer

      The GIL lets only one thread run Python code at a time.

    Takeaway Processes isolate memory and crashes; threads share memory and need locks; in Python use processes for CPU work, threads or asyncio for I/O.

    CPU scheduling#

    PolicyIdeaTrade-off
    FCFSFirst come, first servedShort jobs wait behind long ones (convoy effect)
    SJF / SRTFShortest job (remaining) firstBest average wait, needs run-time estimates, can starve long jobs
    Round robinFixed time slice eachGood response time; slice size trades overhead vs latency
    Priority / MLFQMulti-level queues; demote CPU hogsNeeds ageing to avoid starvation
    Linux CFS / EEVDFFair share of CPU time by weightDefault general-purpose scheduler
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What is the convoy effect?

      Show answer

      Under first-come first-served, short jobs wait behind a long one.

    2. What prevents starvation in priority scheduling?

      Show answer

      Ageing: raising the priority of jobs that have waited long.

    Takeaway FCFS suffers convoys, SJF minimises average wait but starves, round robin trades slice overhead for latency, MLFQ needs ageing.

    Synchronisation and deadlock#

    • Mutex: one holder at a time. Semaphore: a counter allowing N holders. Condition variable: wait until a predicate is true, always in a loop.
    • Race condition: the result depends on interleaving; fix with a lock or an atomic operation around the whole check-then-act.
    • Deadlock needs all four: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, circular wait. Break one, most often circular wait by acquiring locks in a global order.
    • Livelock keeps threads busy without progress; starvation leaves one never served.
    • Prefer higher-level tools (queues, thread pools, actors) over hand-rolled locks.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Name the four deadlock conditions.

      Show answer

      Mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, circular wait.

    2. Why check a condition variable's predicate in a loop?

      Show answer

      Wake-ups can be spurious or the condition can change before the thread runs.

    Takeaway Deadlock needs mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption and circular wait; break circular wait with a global lock order.

    Memory: virtual memory and paging#

    • Each process sees a private virtual address space; the MMU translates pages (typically 4 KB) to physical frames via page tables.
    • The TLB caches recent translations; a TLB miss walks the page table, a page fault loads the page from disk or allocates it.
    • Thrashing: the working set exceeds RAM and the system spends its time paging; fix by adding memory or reducing concurrency.
    • Stack: per thread, automatic, small. Heap: shared, manual or garbage-collected, fragmentation risk.
    • Copy-on-write makes fork() cheap: pages are shared until one side writes.
    • Linux OOM killer ends a process when memory runs out; containers hit their cgroup memory limit first.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why is fork cheap?

      Show answer

      Copy-on-write: pages are shared until one side writes.

    2. What is thrashing?

      Show answer

      The working set exceeds RAM, so the system spends its time paging.

    Takeaway The MMU maps virtual pages to frames via page tables, the TLB caches translations, and thrashing means the working set exceeds RAM.

    I/O models#

    ModelHowUsed by
    BlockingThread waits for each read or writeSimple thread-per-connection servers
    Non-blocking + multiplexingepoll / kqueue tell one thread which sockets are readyNginx, Node.js, Redis, asyncio
    AsynchronousSubmit and get completions (io_uring)High-performance storage and networking

    One thread with an event loop can serve tens of thousands of idle connections; CPU-heavy work still needs more cores or processes.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. How do Nginx, Node and Redis handle many connections?

      Show answer

      Non-blocking I/O with multiplexing (epoll or kqueue) on an event loop.

    2. What can't one event loop fix?

      Show answer

      CPU-heavy work, which still needs more cores or processes.

    Takeaway Blocking needs a thread per connection; epoll/kqueue multiplexing lets one thread serve many sockets; io_uring is truly async.

    Layers, and TCP vs UDP#

    Layer (TCP/IP)Examples
    ApplicationHTTP, DNS, gRPC, WebSocket
    TransportTCP, UDP, QUIC (over UDP)
    InternetIP, ICMP
    LinkEthernet, Wi-Fi
    TCPUDP
    ConnectionHandshake firstNone

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Name the four TCP/IP layers.

      Show answer

      Application, transport, internet (IP), link.

    2. Why does QUIC run over UDP?

      Show answer

      It builds its own reliability on top while avoiding TCP's limitations.

    Takeaway TCP is connection-based, reliable and ordered; UDP is lightweight with no guarantees, suiting DNS, calls, games and QUIC.

    TCP handshake and congestion#

    • Three-way handshake: SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK; one round trip before data. Teardown uses FIN and ACK from each side; TIME_WAIT lingers on the closer.
    • Flow control protects the receiver (advertised window); congestion control protects the network (slow start, then additive increase, multiplicative decrease; CUBIC and BBR in practice).
    • Head-of-line blocking: one lost packet stalls everything behind it on that connection, which is why HTTP/3 moved to QUIC.
    • Latency is bounded by round trips: connection reuse, keep-alive and fewer handshakes matter more than bandwidth for small requests.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What is head-of-line blocking in TCP?

      Show answer

      One lost packet stalls everything behind it on that connection.

    2. For small requests, what dominates latency?

      Show answer

      Round trips, so connection reuse matters more than bandwidth.

    Takeaway SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK costs a round trip; flow control protects the receiver, congestion control the network; one loss blocks the whole TCP stream.

    What happens when you type a URL#

    1. DNS: browser cache → OS cache → recursive resolver → root → TLD → authoritative server returns the IP (cached per TTL).
    2. TCP (or QUIC) connection to the IP, usually port 443, possibly via a CDN edge near you.
    3. TLS handshake: agree on keys, verify the certificate chain (TLS 1.3 takes one round trip).
    4. HTTP request with method, path, headers and cookies; a load balancer routes it to a server.
    5. Server work: application logic, caches, database queries; a response with status, headers and body.
    6. Rendering: the browser parses HTML, fetches CSS, JS and images (often in parallel over HTTP/2 or 3), builds the DOM and paints.

    Interviewers use this to probe depth: be ready to zoom into any step (DNS caching, TLS, load balancing, caching headers).

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What does the recursive resolver ask, in order?

      Show answer

      The root, then the top-level domain, then the authoritative server.

    2. How many round trips does a TLS 1.3 handshake take?

      Show answer

      One.

    Takeaway DNS lookup, TCP or QUIC connect, TLS handshake, HTTP request through a load balancer, server work, then browser parse and paint.

    HTTP versions and TLS#

    VersionKey change
    HTTP/1.1Keep-alive; one request at a time per connection, so browsers open several
    HTTP/2Multiplexed streams over one TCP connection, binary framing, header compression
    HTTP/3Over QUIC (UDP): no TCP head-of-line blocking, faster setup, connection migration
    • TLS gives encryption, integrity and server authentication via certificates signed by a trusted CA; mTLS also authenticates the client.
    • Status codes: 2xx success, 3xx redirect, 4xx client error (400, 401, 403, 404, 429), 5xx server error (500, 502, 503, 504).
    • Idempotent methods: GET, PUT, DELETE (and HEAD, OPTIONS); POST is not.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What problem does HTTP/3 solve that HTTP/2 doesn't?

      Show answer

      TCP head-of-line blocking, by running over QUIC.

    2. What does mutual TLS add?

      Show answer

      It authenticates the client as well as the server.

    Takeaway HTTP/1.1 one request at a time per connection, HTTP/2 multiplexes over TCP, HTTP/3 runs over QUIC; GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent, POST isn't.

    DNS, load balancers and proxies#

    • DNS records: A/AAAA (address), CNAME (alias), MX (mail), TXT (verification), NS (delegation). TTL controls caching and how fast changes spread.
    • L4 load balancer: routes by IP and port, fast, protocol-agnostic. L7: reads HTTP (paths, headers, cookies), can terminate TLS and do canaries.
    • Forward proxy acts for clients; reverse proxy sits in front of servers (Nginx, Envoy).
    • NAT maps private addresses to a public one; CIDR such as /24 means 256 addresses.
    • Common ports: 22 SSH, 53 DNS, 80 HTTP, 443 HTTPS, 5432 Postgres, 6379 Redis, 9092 Kafka.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What can an L7 load balancer do that L4 can't?

      Show answer

      Route by path or header, terminate TLS, run canaries.

    2. Forward vs reverse proxy?

      Show answer

      A forward proxy acts for clients; a reverse proxy sits in front of servers.

    Takeaway L4 balancers route by IP and port; L7 read HTTP to route by path, terminate TLS and run canaries; DNS TTL sets how fast changes spread.

    ACID and isolation levels#

    PropertyMeaning
    AtomicityAll or nothing (undo or write-ahead log)
    ConsistencyConstraints hold before and after
    IsolationConcurrent transactions don't see each other's partial work
    DurabilityCommitted data survives a crash (WAL fsync)
    LevelPreventsStill allows
    Read committedDirty readsNon-repeatable reads, phantoms
    Repeatable read / snapshot+ non-repeatable readsWrite skew
    SerializableAll anomaliesNothing, at a throughput cost
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Which anomaly does snapshot isolation still allow?

      Show answer

      Write skew.

    2. What provides durability after a crash?

      Show answer

      The write-ahead log.

    Takeaway Read committed stops dirty reads; repeatable read/snapshot stops non-repeatable reads but allows write skew; serializable stops all anomalies.

    Normalisation#

    FormRule
    1NFAtomic values, no repeating groups
    2NF1NF + no partial dependency on part of a composite key
    3NF2NF + no transitive dependency (non-key → non-key)
    BCNFEvery determinant is a candidate key

    Normalise to avoid update anomalies; denormalise deliberately for read-heavy paths (and keep the copy in sync with events or triggers).

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What does 3NF remove?

      Show answer

      Transitive dependencies, where a non-key column determines another.

    2. When is denormalising acceptable?

      Show answer

      On read-heavy paths, deliberately, keeping copies in sync with events or triggers.

    Takeaway 1NF atomic values, 2NF no partial key dependency, 3NF no transitive dependency, BCNF every determinant a candidate key; denormalise deliberately.

    Indexes inside the database#

    • B+ tree: sorted, balanced, leaves linked; great for ranges and ordering; the default in Postgres and MySQL.
    • Hash index: equality only. GIN / inverted: full-text and arrays. BRIN: huge, naturally ordered tables (time series).
    • Clustered (InnoDB primary key) stores rows in index order; secondary indexes point back to the row or key.
    • LSM trees (RocksDB, Cassandra) buffer writes in memory and merge sorted files: fast writes, compaction cost.
    • Selectivity matters: an index on a boolean column rarely helps.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. Why do LSM trees give fast writes?

      Show answer

      They buffer writes in memory and merge sorted files later, at the cost of compaction.

    2. Why rarely index a boolean column?

      Show answer

      Low selectivity: the filter matches too many rows to help.

    Takeaway B+ trees for ranges, hash for equality, GIN for text and arrays, BRIN for huge ordered tables; LSM trees trade compaction for fast writes.

    Locking and MVCC#

    • Pessimistic locking: SELECT … FOR UPDATE holds row locks until commit; safe under contention, risks waits and deadlocks.
    • Optimistic concurrency: a version column; update WHERE version = n and retry if zero rows changed.
    • MVCC (Postgres, InnoDB): writers create new row versions, readers see a snapshot, so reads don't block writes. Old versions need cleanup (VACUUM in Postgres).
    • Databases detect deadlocks and abort one transaction; the application must retry.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. How does optimistic concurrency detect conflicts?

      Show answer

      Update only where the version column matches; zero rows updated means retry.

    2. What must the application do about deadlocks?

      Show answer

      Be ready to retry, since the database aborts one transaction.

    Takeaway Pessimistic locks hold rows until commit; optimistic concurrency checks a version; MVCC lets reads not block writes, with VACUUM cleaning old versions.

    SQL vs NoSQL#

    NeedLean towards
    Transactions, joins, constraints, ad-hoc queriesRelational (Postgres, MySQL)
    Huge write volume, key-based access, wide rowsWide-column (Cassandra, DynamoDB)
    Flexible documents, nested dataDocument (MongoDB)
    Caching, counters, leaderboardsKey-value in memory (Redis)
    Relationships as the query (friends of friends)Graph (Neo4j)
    Search, relevanceSearch engine (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch)

    Start relational unless a specific access pattern or scale rules it out; you can add a specialised store later.

    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. When does a wide-column store like Cassandra fit?

      Show answer

      Huge write volumes with key-based access.

    2. Which store suits friends-of-friends queries?

      Show answer

      A graph database.

    Takeaway Default to relational for transactions, joins and ad hoc queries; pick NoSQL for a specific access pattern or scale.

    Replication and sharding in one card#

    • Replication copies data for availability and read scale: leader–follower (simple, replica lag), multi-leader (conflicts), leaderless quorums (R + W > N).
    • Sharding splits data for write scale: by range (good for scans, hot spots) or hash (even, no range queries); consistent hashing eases rebalancing.
    • Read-your-writes after replication: read from the leader for a short window, or track the user's last write position.
    • Full detail lives on the HLD sheet's data section.
    Check yourself 2 questions

    1. What is the leaderless quorum rule?

      Show answer

      Reads plus writes must exceed the replica count (R + W > N).

    2. How do you give read-your-writes with replicas?

      Show answer

      Read from the leader for a short window or track the user's last write position.

    Takeaway Replication scales reads and availability; sharding scales writes; range shards scan well but hot-spot, hash shards spread evenly but lose ranges.
