    01Reading a strategy: the payoff chart and the nine numbers

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Strategies · chapters 1–2 (the strategy builder, the nine numbers)

    The payoff chart: two lines, zones and landmarks#

    • Horizontal axis: NIFTY at expiry. Vertical: rupee P&L for one lot of 65. A strategy's payoff is the sum of its legs at every price.
    • At-expiry line (solid): value once time value is gone, your destination if held. T+0 line (dashed): value today, smoother because time value remains; it converges on the expiry line.
    • Green above zero is the profit zone, red below is loss. Breakevens are where the expiry line crosses zero: one, two or even four of them.
    • Flat shelves mark capped max profit or max loss (defined risk). A vertical line marks spot.
    • Sigma bands: ±1σ (about two-in-three odds of finishing inside) and ±2σ. Compare your profit zone with them: a zone outside the outer band is a bet on a rare move.

    Read any payoff in six steps#

    1. Find the zero line.
    2. Mark the breakeven dots.
    3. Name the green and red zones.
    4. Note the flat max-profit and max-loss shelves, or where a side is open.
    5. Locate spot.
    6. Lay the profit zone over the sigma bands: is the needed move plausible?

    Worked read, bull call spread (buy 24,050 call, sell 24,150 call): loss shelf ₹3,624 below 24,050, profit shelf ₹2,876 above 24,150, breakeven 24,106 inside the inner band, so it bets on a modest, plausible rise. A sold leg always means an assignment obligation.

    Compare a tight credit iron condor (inner strikes 23,950/24,150): max profit ₹5,616, max loss ₹884, POP 17%. Its debit mirror makes ₹884, risks ₹5,616 and wins 83% of the time. High POP comes with small reward and vice versa: the market prices trades so no shape is free money.

    Rules that always hold#

    credit + max loss = strike width × lot (credit spread)debit + max profit = strike width × lot (debit spread)call-side breakeven = strike + premium per shareput-side breakeven = strike − premium per share100-point width × 65 = ₹6,500
    • Read POP and reward together, never one alone.
    • Size by max loss, never by the credit or the premium turnover.
    • Margin is cash locked while the trade is open; count it as a cost of capital.

    Where "unlimited" really is#

    PositionLoss sideSize
    Naked short call, short futureRallyUnlimited
    Naked short put, long futureFallBounded by zero, but huge (≈ ₹15 lakh a NIFTY lot)
    Any bought optionEitherThe premium, no more

    An "Unlimited" label is a flag, not the whole story: identify the realistic move that would hurt, and how fast it could come.

    Bull call spread#

    buy 24,050 call (425.8) · sell 24,150 call (370.1)net debit 55.7 pts → ₹3,624 = max loss (at/below 24,050)max profit = ₹6,500 − ₹3,624 = ₹2,876 (at/above 24,150)breakeven = 24,050 + 55.7 ≈ 24,106
    • POP ≈ 47% against 31% for the lone call (breakeven 24,476): better odds bought by capping the upside.
    • Margin ≈ ₹35,378 (exposure on the sold leg), far above the ₹3,624 real risk; return on margin ≈ 8% if it works.
    • Less time value than a lone call, so decay is milder. Sell the strike you realistically expect NIFTY to reach: too close caps the gain early, too far adds cost for little.

    Bull put spread and the width dial#

    Spread (sell / buy put)CreditMax lossBreakevenPOPMarginReturn on margin
    50 pts (24,050 / 24,000)₹1,173₹2,07724,03250%₹33,6333.5%
    100 pts (24,050 / 23,950)₹2,506₹3,99424,01151%₹35,3587.1%

    • Sell the higher put, buy the lower one: a credit trade that wins if NIFTY simply does not fall below the short strike. Time decay works for you.
    • Formulas: max profit = credit; max loss = width − credit; breakeven = short strike − credit per share. A naked short put would need over ₹1.6 lakh of margin; the bought put cuts that to ≈ ₹35,000.
    • Wider spreads pay more and risk more rupees; a higher short strike pays more but needs NIFTY to rise a little.
    • Bull call (debit) vs bull put (credit): pay up front and hope to be right, or be paid up front and hope to be left alone. Prefer the put spread when put premiums and IV are rich (often after a fall), the call spread when premiums are cheap and markets calm.
    • Defined risk is at expiry: it does not stop an overnight gap from showing the full loss on screen, or margin calls along the way. One crash can hand back months of credits; avoid it before a breakdown or a big event.

    All three have unlimited upside; they differ in what they risk to get it. The back spread hides its loss in the middle (a small rise that stalls at the bought strike) and is the only one with a defined worst case. The synthetic moves point for point like a long future with almost no time value; the range forward has a flat dead zone between its strikes, then behaves like a long future below 23,950. Their margins of ≈ ₹1.7 lakh, against ≈ ₹52,000, are the warning sign.

    • Buy the higher put, sell the lower: a debit trade for a measured fall. Breakeven = higher strike − debit per share, 45 points under spot and inside the inner sigma band.

    • Breakevens = strike ∓ total premium per share. The straddle's band is 1,442 points wide: roughly the ±720-point move the market already prices in.
    • Expiry checks (long side): straddle at 23,000 → +₹21,392, at 25,300 → +₹34,392; strangle +₹21,021 and +₹34,021. The short versions lose exactly those amounts.
    • Shapes: the straddle is a sharp V (long) or an inverted V (short); the strangle is a flat-bottomed bucket or a flat tabletop.

    Long straddle and strangle: buying a big move#

    • A bet on size, not direction. The enemy is a calm or slowly drifting market: theta hits both legs every session.
    • Straddle: tighter breakevens, higher cost, pays sooner. Strangle: about ₹6,100 cheaper but needs a bigger move and loses the full debit anywhere between its strikes.
    • POP under 50% is normal: the premium already prices a fair move. The real move must beat it.
    • Don't buy after a known event has inflated IV, and don't sit through a slow drift: exit when the move fails to come.

    Short straddle and strangle: selling calm#

    • The credit is the max profit, kept only if NIFTY stays inside the band; both sold legs are naked, so loss is open-ended either way.
    • Return on margin looks juicy (≈ 29% straddle, ≈ 25% strangle) and POP is near 60%, but one rare loss can exceed many small wins.
    • Write the exit before entry: close after a large part of the credit has decayed, or when a breakeven is threatened. Don't wait for expiry.
    • Buying further-out wings turns them into a defined-risk iron fly or iron condor. Learn these naked versions to respect the risk, then fence the tails.

    Iron condor = bull put spread + bear call spread on one expiry. Names differ by platform: OpenAlgo's builder calls the credit face the "long" iron condor, while many platforms call it "short". Read the payoff and the nine numbers, never the label.

    Strikes are an odds dial#

    • Inner strikes close to spot (as above): a big credit, a narrow band, low POP. The ±720-point expected move dwarfs a 200-point band, which is why the credit face wins only 17% of the time.
    • Inner strikes further out: a wider band, higher POP, smaller credit. Classic income condors sell well out of the money.
    • A credit is not automatically good, nor a debit bad: they are the same four legs with opposite bets on where NIFTY lands.
    • Defined risk is not small risk: judge by the printed max loss and margin.

    StructureLegs (sell Jul, buy Aug)DebitMax profitMax lossBreakevensPOPMargin
    Call calendarSell 24,050 CE 425.8 · buy Aug 24,050 CE 627.8₹13,130₹14,480 at 24,050 on 28 Jul≈ ₹13,09023,495 / 24,634≈ 47%≈ ₹58,373

    • Long vega through the back leg: a fall in IV shrinks it and can lose money even with spot unchanged. Suits calm, event-free months; avoid entering just before a scheduled shock.
    • Margins of ≈ ₹49,000–58,000 against lakhs for naked shorts.

    long call + short put (same strike) = long futureshort call + long put (same strike) = short futurelong future + short higher call = covered call ≈ short put
    • Long synthetic at 24,050: debit ₹8,496, breakeven 24,181. Expiry: 22,000 → −₹1,41,746 · 25,000 → +₹53,255 · 26,000 → +₹1,18,255. Little time value, so the T+0 and expiry lines nearly overlap.
    • The short synthetic is its mirror: credit ₹8,496, unlimited loss on a rally, margin ≈ ₹1,69,046.
    • Both block over ₹1.6 lakh and carry futures-grade risk through their naked leg. A small credit is not low danger.

    The covered call#

    • Hold the underlying (here a long NIFTY future) and sell a higher call: 24,150 call ≈ ₹24,057 a lot.
    • Below 24,150 at expiry you keep the premium; above it your upside stops at the strike plus the premium.
    • The premium is only a thin cushion: the full downside of the holding remains. It does not protect against a fall.
    • Suits a sideways-to-mildly-higher view on a holding you are happy to give up at the strike; don't write calls on shares you never want to sell.

    07Funding: margin, collateral, pledging and penalties

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Strategies · chapters 22–24 (margin and collateral, pledging for margin, margin penalties)

    Margin: margin the whole structure#

    • Buyers pay the premium and are done. Sellers and futures legs carry open-ended risk, so the exchange blocks SPAN (its worst one-day move for your exact position) plus exposure margin (a buffer for an unusual day) until the position closes.
    • Risk sets the margin, not the premium collected.
    • Enter every leg in a margin calculator together: hedged structures are margined on their capped risk, so a condor or butterfly needs far less than the naked short inside it. Defined risk is cheaper to hold, not just safer.
    • Cash drag: a short strangle can tie up ≈ ₹1.5 lakh that earns nothing; across positions, lakhs sit idle. Pledging is the fix.

    Collateral, haircuts and the 50% cash rule#

    Collateral (approx.)HaircutMargin per ₹100Counts as
    Cash ETFs≈ 6%≈ ₹94Cash-equivalent
    G-Secs, SGBs, T-bills, liquid funds≈ 10%≈ ₹90Cash-equivalent
    Stable large stock≈ 9% (another chapter quotes 20–25%)≈ ₹75–91Non-cash
    Volatile stock18%+ and variable≤ ₹82Non-cash
    • Pledged securities stay in your demat; you keep dividends and bonuses. Only the post-haircut value counts as margin.
    • At least half of F&O margin must be cash or cash-equivalents; the rest may be stocks, equity ETFs or funds. Daily mark-to-market losses are always settled in cash.
    • Example: ≈ ₹1,00,000 of a liquid fund (near full value, covers the cash half) + ≈ ₹1,00,000 of stock (≈ ₹75,000 after haircut) funds a ≈ ₹1.5 lakh requirement with no cash frozen.
    • MTF (buying shares with borrowed money) has its own, narrower collateral list (ETFs and stocks); a SEBI consultation proposes widening it. Haircuts and lists change, so check them before pledging.

    Pledging smartly, and the forced-sale trap#

    cash-shortfall interest ≈ 0.035% a day ≈ 12.8% a year₹50,000 short of cash ≈ ₹17.5 a daypledge ₹2,00,000 RELIANCE, 9% haircut → ₹1,82,000 marginstock −10% → ₹1,63,800 → shortfall ₹18,200 → margin call
    • If the call isn't met, the broker sells pledged shares at market to cover it. Forced selling, not the haircut, is the real danger, and it hits your portfolio, not just the trade.
    • Prefer liquid collateral: ₹1,00,000 at a 6% haircut gives ₹94,000; a volatile stock at 20% gives ₹80,000, and only ≈ ₹64,000 after a 20% fall.
    • Use liquid assets for the cash half and volatile stock only as a small top-up.
    • Covered call on pledged shares: 500 RELIANCE (≈ ₹6.59 lakh, ≈ ₹6 lakh margin after haircut), sell a 1360 call at ≈ ₹16 for ≈ ₹8,000 of premium, with no new directional bet. Without a cash leg it still pays shortfall interest.
    • Pledging is usually credited the same day; unpledging is free; selling a pledged share unpledges it automatically.

    Peak margin and penalty slabs#

    ShortfallPenalty on the shortfall
    Under ₹1 lakh0.5% (₹50,000 short → ₹250)
    ₹1 lakh or more1% (₹2,00,000 short → ₹2,000)
    More than 3 times in a monthUp to 5%
    • Minimum margin is exchange-set and the same at every broker: SPAN + exposure for F&O, VAR + ELM for equity. No broker can offer extra intraday leverage.
    • The clearing corporation checks margin at several random intraday snapshots plus end of day. Being short at any one snapshot counts, so topping up just before the close doesn't help.
    • Upfront shortfalls (at entry) are the broker's problem, so you'll be blocked from entering. Non-upfront ones (futures MTM due T+1, expiry-week delivery margins, ad hoc margins on stock derivatives) can be passed to you.
    • Penalties go to the clearing corporation's core settlement guarantee fund.

    Order of work: form a specific view, pick the payoff that rewards it, size it, set exits. Never pick a strategy first and bend the view to fit.     Size from the loss limit#

    loss limit = account × 1–2%lots = loss limit ÷ max loss per lot (under 1 → skip)₹5,00,000 × 2% = ₹10,000bull call spread ₹3,624/lot → 2 lots = ₹7,248 ✓long straddle ₹46,858/lot → skip

    Size by max loss, never by confidence or by the credit. Small enough to survive a losing streak is the goal.

    Management and exit rules#

    • Credit trades: take profit at roughly 50–75% of max profit; don't squeeze out the last rupees.
    • Debit trades: close once the expected move has mostly happened and the position is near max profit.
    • Exit when the reason for the trade is gone, at a pre-set loss level, or on a pre-set date. Write profit, stop and exit date before entry.
    • Repairs stay simple: roll the untested side closer, or move a threatened short strike further away. Never widen risk to avoid a loss.

    The adjustment playbook#

    MoveWhat it doesTrade-off
    Roll out in timeClose the near legs, reopen the same structure in a later expiryOften a net credit, but the open risk lasts longer
    Roll a strike up or down (same expiry)Moves a tested strike further from priceUsually costs part of the credit
    Widen a spreadShifts a tested spread to safer strikesStays defined-risk
    Add the opposite spreadTurns a credit spread into an iron condorMore credit and a wider zone; doubles exposure if rushed
    Buy a protective wingCaps a naked short, making it a spreadGives back some credit; usually cuts margin
    • Valid reasons: shrink a loss into something smaller or defined, buy time when the view is right but late, or re-centre when the view has genuinely changed. Not valid: refusing to be wrong, or adding risk to claw a loss back.
    • Examples: a short strangle (23,950 put / 24,150 call) leaning on the call after two weeks → roll the tested call up, or roll the whole structure out a month. A bull put spread left behind by a rally → add a bear call spread above to form an iron condor.
    • Workflow: record the closed leg's exit price, add the new leg, re-check max profit, max loss, breakevens and margin, and rehearse in the sandbox first. Capping a naked short usually lowers margin; rolling out or adding a short side usually raises it.

    Position Greeks: add up the legs#

    Net GreekTells youShort straddle
    DeltaDirectional lean (≈ 0 is neutral)≈ 0
    GammaWhether moves help more and more (long) or hurt more and more (short)Short
    ThetaDaily decay earned (+) or paid (−)Positive
    VegaGains when IV falls (short) or rises (long)Short

    Bought legs add, sold legs subtract. The payoff chart shows expiry; Greeks show the path before it. A credit iron condor has the same signature as the short straddle with its loss capped. Choose the vega sign deliberately: it decides whether you are a buyer or seller of volatility.

    Reading the volatility regime#

    RegimePremiumLeanExamples
    High IV rank / India VIXRichSell (short vega)Credit iron condor; short strangle for experts
    Low IV rank / VIXCheapBuy (long vega)Debit spreads, long options
    Edge in the curve shapeMixedCalendarSell near, buy far
    • ATM IV of 12.7% means nothing alone. With a 10–22% yearly range it ranks low; IV percentile says how often IV has been below today's level.
    • India VIX: the market's expected ~30-day NIFTY volatility from the whole chain. Low and falling: calm, slow decay. High and rising: fat premium, more danger.
    • Skew: index puts carry higher IV than equally distant calls, so put wings pay more per point. The two sides of a range trade are not the same bet.
    • Term structure: normally far months carry higher IV; an inverted curve (near above far) flags event premium that should collapse afterwards.
    • "Sell high, buy low" is a starting bias, not a rule: high IV may reflect real risk, so check the calendar first.

    Strategy pre-trade checklist#

    1. Can I state my view in one sentence, with a timeframe?
    2. Does this payoff actually reward that view?
    3. Do I know the breakevens, max profit and max loss?
    4. Have I read POP next to max loss?
    5. Is the worst case defined?
    6. Does the lot count come from my loss limit (1–2% of capital)?
    7. Does the margin fit with a buffer, and what is my unwind order?
    8. Are profit target, stop and exit date written down?
    9. Have I rehearsed it in the builder or sandbox?
