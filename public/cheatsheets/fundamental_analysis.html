    How to use this sheet#

    1. Learning: statements first, then ratios, then valuation. Valuation without reading the statements is guesswork.
    2. Researching a stock: follow the workflow below; use the ladders to pick a valuation method and to screen out value traps and weak earnings.
    3. Before buying: run the last-minute checklist and write down your sell rules.
    4. Ongoing: re-read the thesis every quarter when results come out.
    Educational, not investment advice. Built from your notes for study; figures in examples are illustrative.

    01Foundations: value, price and investing styles

    TradingOverview/fundamental-analysis/summary · book-notes (The Intelligent Investor, Security Analysis, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits)

    What fundamental analysis does#

    • Estimates a business's intrinsic value from its cash flows, assets, growth and risk, then compares it with the market price.
    • Price can stray from value for years; over long periods it tends to follow earnings and cash flow.
    • Buy when price is well below value (margin of safety); avoid or sell when price is far above value or quality is deteriorating.
    • Fundamentals decide what to own; technicals help decide when.

    Graham's core ideas#

    • Mr. Market offers you a price every day, sometimes euphoric, sometimes depressed. Use his moods; never take orders from them.
    • Investment vs speculation: an investment, on thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return.
    • Margin of safety: the gap between value and price absorbs your mistakes and bad luck.
    • Defensive investor: diversified, high-quality, low-effort. Enterprising investor: more work for bargains and special situations.
    • The investor's chief problem, and worst enemy, is likely to be himself.

    Investing styles compared#

    StyleLooks forTypical metricsMain riskAssociated with
    Deep valueAssets or earnings far below priceLow P/B, P/E, EV/EBIT; net-netsValue trapsGraham
    Quality / compoundersHigh ROIC with a long reinvestment runwayROIC, FCF conversion, moatOverpayingBuffett, Munger, Terry Smith
    GrowthFast, durable revenue and earnings growthRevenue and EPS growth, TAM, unit economicsGrowth stalls; multiple collapseFisher, Lynch
    GARPGrowth at a reasonable pricePEG around 1 or belowMediocre at bothLynch
    Dividend / incomeSustainable, growing payoutsYield, FCF coverage, payout ratioDividend cuts—
    Special situationsEvent-driven mispricingSpin-offs, mergers, restructuringsDeal or event riskGreenblatt

    Two ways to value anything (Damodaran)#

    IntrinsicRelative
    QuestionWhat are the cash flows worth?What do similar assets sell for?
    ToolsDCF, DDM, EPVP/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B, comps
    StrengthForces you to state assumptionsMarket-grounded, fast
    WeaknessSensitive to inputsInherits market mispricing

    Every valuation is biased (you choose the inputs), uncertain, and simpler is often better. Use both and look for agreement.

    Where to find the numbers (India)#

    • Annual reports and quarterly results: NSE and BSE corporate filings, company investor-relations pages.
    • Ten-year financials and screens: Screener.in (export to Excel), Tickertape, Trendlyne.
    • Management commentary: earnings-call transcripts and investor presentations.
    • Shareholding, pledges, insider trades: quarterly shareholding pattern and SAST/PIT disclosures on the exchanges.
    • Credit ratings: CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings rationales explain debt risks plainly.
    • Macro: RBI (DBIE, policy statements), MoSPI (CPI, IIP, GDP).

    02Financial statements: reading the big three

    TradingOverview/fundamental-analysis/handbook/01-financial-statements · book-notes/financial-statements-step-by-step-guide (Ittelson)

    How the three statements connect#

    flowchart LR
  IS["Income statement: net income"] --> CFO["Cash flow: operating section starts from net income"]
  IS --> RE["Balance sheet: retained earnings grow by profit less dividends"]
  WC["Balance sheet: change in receivables, inventory, payables"] --> CFO
  CFO --> CASH["Balance sheet: ending cash"]
  CAPEX["Cash flow: capex in investing"] --> FA["Balance sheet: fixed assets"]
  DEP["Income statement: depreciation"] --> FA
  FIN["Cash flow: debt and equity raised or repaid"] --> DEBT["Balance sheet: debt and equity"]

    Income statement#

    Revenue (net sales)
− Cost of goods sold
= Gross profit                  → gross margin
− Operating expenses (SG&A, R&D, depreciation)
= Operating income (EBIT)       → operating margin
± Interest and other income
= Profit before tax
− Tax
= Net income                    → net margin
÷ Diluted shares
= Diluted EPS
    • Separate organic growth (volume, price) from acquisitions.
    • Operating leverage: % change in EBIT ÷ % change in revenue.
    • Strip one-offs to get normalised earnings; "recurring one-offs" are costs.
    • India: watch "other income" (treasury gains) and exceptional items.

    Balance sheet#

    assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity
    ItemWhat to check
    Cash and investmentsReal and available? Net cash vs debt
    ReceivablesGrowing faster than sales? DSO trend
    InventoryGrowing faster than COGS? DIO trend, write-downs
    Fixed assets, CWIPCapital-work-in-progress that never gets commissioned
    Goodwill and intangiblesAbove 30–40% of assets = acquisition risk
    DebtMaturity schedule, fixed vs floating, covenants
    Deferred revenueHigh-quality liability for subscription firms
    Contingent liabilitiesFootnotes: guarantees, tax disputes, litigation
    net debt = total debt − cash and liquid investmentstangible book = equity − goodwill − intangibles

    Cash flow statement#

    SectionContainsHealthy pattern
    Operating (CFO)Net income + non-cash items ± working capitalPositive, tracking or above net income
    Investing (CFI)Capex, acquisitions, investmentsNegative for a growing firm
    Financing (CFF)Debt, equity, dividends, buybacksNegative for a mature cash generator

    "Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality." The cash flow statement is the hardest to manipulate.

    Free cash flow variants#

    FCF = CFO − capexFCFF = EBIT × (1 − t) + D&A − capex − ΔNWCFCFE = net income + D&A − capex − ΔNWC + net borrowingFCF margin = FCF ÷ revenue (above 10% = capital-efficient)FCF yield = FCF ÷ market cap (above 5% = potentially attractive)dividend cover = FCF ÷ dividends (above 1.5× = sustainable)

    For tech companies, also subtract stock-based compensation: it is a real cost paid in shares.

    Working-capital days#

    DSO = receivables ÷ revenue × 365DIO = inventory ÷ COGS × 365DPO = payables ÷ COGS × 365cash conversion cycle = DSO + DIO − DPO
    • A shorter cycle frees cash; a negative cycle means suppliers fund the business (strong retailers, some FMCG).
    • A rising DSO is an early warning of aggressive revenue recognition or weak customers.
    • A rise in receivables or inventory uses cash; a rise in payables or deferred revenue releases it.

    Capex intensity by industry#

    IndustryCapex / revenue
    Software, asset-light tech2–5%
    Consumer staples3–6%
    Healthcare, pharma5–10%
    Manufacturing5–12%
    Semiconductors10–20%
    Telecom, cable15–25%
    Airlines, rail15–25%
    Oil and gas exploration20–40%

    Capex persistently below depreciation (without shrinking) usually means under-investment.

    Statement red flags#

    StatementRed flagWhy it matters
    IncomeProfit growing while CFO is flat or fallingAccrual earnings; usually resolves against the stock
    IncomeBig swings in gross margin quarter to quarterPossible channel stuffing or one-off pricing
    IncomeProfit driven by other income or asset salesWeak core business hidden
    IncomeSudden fall in effective tax rateNon-operational EPS boost
    BalanceReceivables or inventory growing faster than salesCollection or demand problems
    BalanceDebt maturities bunched in 1–3 yearsRefinancing risk
    BalanceLarge loans or advances to related partiesCash leaking to the promoter group
    Cash flowPositive FCF only from squeezing working capitalNot repeatable
    Cash flowOperations funded by repeated debt or equity raisesCash burn; dilution
    Cash flowInterest capitalised into assetsUnderstates the true cost of debt

    Ittelson's 12 accounting principles#

    1. Entity: the company is separate from its owners.
    2. Going concern: assumed to keep operating.
    3. Measurement: only objective, verifiable data.
    4. Units of measure: recorded in money.
    5. Historical cost: assets at what was paid.
    6. Materiality: report what would change a reader's decision.
    7. Estimates and judgements: unavoidable, so read them critically.
    8. Consistency: same methods year to year.
    9. Conservatism: record losses when probable, gains when realised.
    10. Periodicity: results cut into periods.
    11. Substance over form: economics beat legal labels.
    12. Accrual basis: revenue when earned, costs when incurred.

    Reading an annual report in order#

    1. Chairman's / MD's letter: strategy and tone; compare with last year's promises.
    2. Management discussion and analysis: drivers, segments, risks.
    3. Cash flow statement before the P&L.
    4. Notes to accounts: policies, contingent liabilities, related parties, debt terms.
    5. Auditor's report: qualifications, emphasis of matter, key audit matters.
    6. Segment data and consolidated vs standalone numbers.
    7. Corporate governance report: board, pay, attendance, related-party policy.

    Profitability and return ratios#

    RatioFormulaGood sign

    DuPont decomposition#

    flowchart LR
  ROE["ROE"] --> NM["Net margin = net income / sales"]
  ROE --> AT["Asset turnover = sales / assets"]
  ROE --> EM["Equity multiplier = assets / equity"]
  NM --> TB["Tax burden = net income / PBT"]
  NM --> IB["Interest burden = PBT / EBIT"]
  NM --> OM["Operating margin = EBIT / sales"]
    ROE = margin × turnover × leverage5-factor ROE = tax burden × interest burden × operating margin × asset turnover × leverage

    Same ROE, different quality#

    DriverSoftware firmRetailer
    Net margin25%3%
    Asset turnover0.8×3.0×
    Equity multiplier1.0×2.2×
    ROE20%≈20%

    The software firm earns its ROE from margins with no debt; the retailer needs turnover and leverage, so a small margin dip hurts far more. ROE built on leverage is fragile.

    ROIC vs WACC: the value test#

    • ROIC > WACC: every rupee reinvested creates value; growth is good.
    • ROIC = WACC: growth adds nothing.
    • ROIC < WACC: growth destroys value; the faster it grows, the worse.
    • Track incremental ROIC (ΔNOPAT ÷ Δinvested capital) to see whether new investment earns as much as the old.
    reinvestment rate = (capex − D&A + ΔNWC) ÷ NOPATgrowth ≈ reinvestment rate × ROIC

    Ratio habits#

    • Compare with peers and with the company's own 5–10 year history, never in isolation.
    • Use averages of opening and closing balances for return ratios.
    • Use the same data source and definitions across companies.
    • Look at the trend: a 12% ROE rising from 6% beats an 18% ROE falling from 30%.
    • Banks and insurers need their own ratios (see sector KPIs).
    • Negative equity from buybacks breaks D/E; use net debt / EBITDA instead.

    WACC and CAPM#

    cost of equity Re = Rf + β × ERPWACC = E/V × Re + D/V × Rd × (1 − t)
    Input (illustrative, India)Value
    Risk-free rate (10-year G-sec)7.0%
    Beta1.1
    Equity risk premium6.0%
    Cost of equity7 + 1.1 × 6 = 13.6%
    Pre-tax cost of debt / tax9% / 25% → 6.75% after tax
    Weights E / D80% / 20%
    WACC0.8 × 13.6 + 0.2 × 6.75 = 12.2%

    Use market-value or target weights. Add a premium for small, illiquid companies. Keep terminal g below the risk-free rate and nominal GDP growth.

    DCF mistakes#

    • High growth forever; every business fades towards GDP growth.
    • Assuming today's peak margins persist; high returns attract competition.
    • WACC too low, inflating value. Treat WACC as a range.
    • Ignoring dilution from stock options and future equity raises.
    • Exit multiple taken from a market peak.
    • Terminal value above 80% of the total: a faith-based model.
    • Growth without reinvestment: growth must be paid for (growth ≈ reinvestment rate × ROIC).
    • No sensitivity or scenario analysis.

    Relative valuation: comps#

    1. Peer set: 4–8 tight peers with the same end market, business model, size (0.3–3× market cap), growth and margins.
    2. Spread multiples: EV/Sales, EV/EBITDA, P/E, P/FCF on trailing and forward numbers.
    3. Statistics: median and quartiles (the median resists outliers).
    4. Position the company: premium for better growth, returns or moat; discount for leverage, governance or concentration.
    5. Football field: plot ranges from each method; the overlap is the estimate.

    Precedent transactions run 15–40% above trading comps because acquirers pay a control premium (typically 20–40%) and synergies.

    The DCF in Python#

    def dcf(rev0, growth, margins, tax=0.25, da_pct=0.04, capex_pct=0.06, nwc_pct=0.10,
        wacc=0.11, g=0.05, net_debt=300, shares=50):
    """Unlevered DCF in ₹ crore. Returns (value per share, details)."""
    rev, pv, rows = rev0, 0.0, []
    for t, (gr, m) in enumerate(zip(growth, margins), start=1):
        new_rev = rev * (1 + gr)
        ebit = new_rev * m
        nopat = ebit * (1 - tax)
        fcff = nopat + da_pct * new_rev - capex_pct * new_rev - nwc_pct * (new_rev - rev)
        pv += fcff / (1 + wacc) ** t
        rows.append((t, new_rev, ebit, nopat, fcff))
        rev = new_rev
    tv = rows[-1][4] * (1 + g) / (wacc - g)          # Gordon growth on final-year FCFF
    pv_tv = tv / (1 + wacc) ** len(rows)
    ev = pv + pv_tv
    equity = ev - net_debt
    return equity / shares, dict(rows=rows, pv_fcff=pv, tv=tv, pv_tv=pv_tv, ev=ev, equity=equity)

value, d = dcf(1000, [0.12, 0.11, 0.10, 0.09, 0.08], [0.18, 0.185, 0.19, 0.195, 0.20])
print(round(value, 1))                                # 46.2

def implied_growth(price, margins, lo=-0.2, hi=0.6):   # reverse DCF by bisection
    for _ in range(80):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if dcf(1000, [mid] * 5, margins)[0] < price:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return mid

    Margin of safety#

    MOS = (intrinsic value − price) ÷ intrinsic valueupside to value = intrinsic value ÷ price − 1
    Business qualityRequired MOS
    Wide moat, predictable earnings20–25%
    Average quality30–40%
    Low quality, cyclical or distressed40–50%+
    Deep value, special situations50%+

    Example: value ₹100, price ₹65 → MOS 35%, upside 54%. In the DCF example, a price of ₹35 against ₹46.2 is a 24% MOS.

    Price vs quality matrix#

    High-quality businessLow-quality business
    Price far below valueIdeal: buy, size upPossible, with a strict MOS and catalyst
    Price near valueAcceptable for compounders; holdAvoid
    Price above valueOnly if growth far exceeds your caseDefinitely avoid

    Buffett's evolution of Graham: "a wonderful company at a fair price" beats "a fair company at a wonderful price".

    Value trap or genuine bargain?#

    Value trap signsGenuine undervaluation signs
    Revenue falling for several years in a rowEarnings temporarily depressed at a cyclical trough
    Gross margins eroding year after yearMarket overreacting to one bad quarter
    Industry being disruptedSector-wide sell-off dragging good companies down
    Management repeatedly missing guidanceHidden assets (land, stakes, patents) not in earnings
    Peers growing while it shrinksManagement buying back stock or insiders buying
    Cheap only because of heavy debtStrong FCF despite weak reported profit (non-cash charges)
    "Cheap on book" with a low ROE and no catalystClear catalyst: new product, restructuring, demerger

    Is this cheap stock a trap?#

    Stock looks cheap on multiples
    1Revenue falling for several years?
    yes
    Likely trapstructural decline: needs a catalyst or pass
    no ↓
    2Margins and ROE falling while peers hold up?
    yes
    Likely trapcompetitive position eroding
    no ↓
    3Debt heavy relative to cash flow?
    yes
    Riskyrefinancing or dilution can wipe out equity
    no ↓
    4Cheap because of a temporary or cyclical problem?
    yes
    Possible bargainvalue on normalised earnings with a margin of safety
    no
    Investigatefind out why the market disagrees before buying

    Using the margin of safety#

    • Value first, price second: estimate value before looking at the quote to avoid anchoring.
    • Conservative inputs: build value from the bear-to-base case, not the bull case.
    • Size with the margin: a wide margin and a confident estimate justify a bigger position.
    • Dynamic: crashes offer wide margins; in bull markets don't lower the bar just to be invested.
    • Not a stop-loss: a falling price with stable value widens the margin; a falling value is the real warning.
    • Options angle: selling a put at your target buy price is one way to be paid while waiting.

    Five sources of economic moat#

    SourceHow it protects profitsEvidence in the numbersHow it erodesExamples
    Brand / intangiblesCustomers pay a premium for trust or statusGross margin above peers; price rises without volume lossQuality failures, cultural shiftsApple, Coca-Cola, luxury houses
    Cost advantageScale, process or location gives the lowest unit costMargins at the top of the industry while pricing lowA rival reaches the same scale or new technologyCostco, Nucor
    Network effectsEach user makes the product more valuable for othersFalling churn, rising engagement, failed challengersPlatform shifts, regulationVisa, Mastercard, exchanges
    Switching costsLeaving costs money, time or riskRetention above 90%, net revenue retention above 100%Easier migration tools, open standardsERP and core banking software
    Efficient scale / licencesA market that fits only one or two players, or regulated entryStable local dominance, high returns without new entrantsDeregulation, patent expiryAirports, pipelines, rating agencies

    Testing a moat#

    • Numbers: ROIC above WACC (15%+) for 10+ years, stable gross margins through downturns, pricing power in inflation.
    • One sentence: can you say why customers pay this company and not a rival? If not, there may be no moat.
    • The ₹-crore test: if a well-funded rival spent heavily to take share, would it fail (wide), dent (narrow) or win (none)?
    • Direction: is the moat widening or narrowing?
    • Wide moat: lasts 20+ years. Narrow: 10–20 years. None: returns fall to the cost of capital within a few years.

    Porter's five forces#

    ForceHurts profits when
    Threat of new entrantsLow capital needs, no scale, licences or brand barriers
    Supplier powerFew critical suppliers; input is a large share of cost
    Buyer powerFew large buyers, commodity product, low switching costs
    SubstitutesAnother product meets the need at a better price-performance
    RivalryMany similar rivals, slow growth, high fixed costs, price wars

    Best industries: all five favourable (payment networks, exchanges, rating agencies). Worst: airlines, where all five are adverse.

    Industry life cycle#

    StageLooks likeInvestor focus
    IntroductionUnproven technology, losses, competing standardsVenture-like risk; most fail
    GrowthRevenue +20–50%, margins building, shake-out beginsWho wins the consolidation; unit economics
    MaturityGDP-like growth, a few leaders, high returns for leadersMoat durability, dividends, buybacks
    DeclineStructural shrinkage, exitsCash harvest only; avoid debt-laden players

    Capital allocation: the CEO's real job#

    Use of cashCreates value whenDestroys value when
    Reinvest organicallyIncremental ROIC above WACCYears of capex with flat ROIC
    AcquisitionsFair price, clear fit, integration recordEmpire building at peak prices; impairments later (50–70% of deals fail to add value)
    DividendsNo high-return reinvestment leftFunded by debt while the business weakens
    BuybacksPrice below intrinsic valueAt peak prices, debt-funded, or only offsetting option dilution
    Debt paydownLeverage high or rates rising—

    Management and governance scorecard#

    Dimension (score 1–5)Questions
    Capital allocationROIC trend, M&A returns, buyback timing
    CommunicationCandid about problems? Specific guidance? Admits mistakes?
    AlignmentPromoter or insider ownership; pay tied to ROIC, not just EPS
    GovernanceIndependent board and audit committee; related-party discipline
    ExecutionDelivered against guidance over 5+ years?
    Long-term orientationConsistent strategy; not managed quarter to quarter

    Governance red flags#

    • Goodwill rising with no improvement in ROIC (empire building).
    • CFO or auditor changes without explanation.
    • Large related-party transactions or loans to group companies.
    • Insiders selling heavily while talking up the stock.
    • "Non-recurring" charges every year; results that always beat by a whisker.
    • Earnings calls: blaming macro when peers don't, new "key metrics", vague answers, abandoned strategies.
    • Pay that rises while returns fall; repriced options.

    Fisher's 15 points (Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits)#

    #Question#Question
    1Products with market potential for sizeable sales growth for years?9Depth of management?
    2Management determined to develop new products as current ones mature?10Good cost analysis and accounting controls?
    3Effective R&D for its size?11Industry-specific advantages over competitors?
    4Above-average sales organisation?12Long-range rather than short-range outlook on profits?
    5Worthwhile profit margin?13Will growth need equity financing that dilutes holders?
    6Doing something to maintain or improve margins?14Does management talk freely in bad times, not only good?
    7Outstanding labour and personnel relations?15Management of unquestionable integrity?
    8Outstanding executive relations?

    Scuttlebutt: talk to customers, suppliers, competitors and ex-employees. Fisher's three reasons to sell: the original purchase was a mistake; the company no longer meets the 15 points; a much better opportunity exists (rarely). Never sell just because the market might fall.

    Normalised EPS#

    • Add back genuine one-offs: restructuring, impairments, deal costs, litigation settlements.
    • Remove one-off gains: asset sales, tax windfalls, treasury gains.
    • Average "one-time" charges that recur over 3–5 years and treat them as costs.
    • Keep stock-based compensation as a cost even when management's adjusted EPS excludes it.
    • Cyclicals: use mid-cycle margins, not peak or trough.

    08Earnings season playbook

    TradingOverview/fundamental-analysis/handbook/06-earnings-analysis/earnings-season-playbook

    Calendar#

    MarketRuleSeason
    India (SEBI LODR)Quarterly results within 45 days of quarter end; annual audited results within 60 days of year endJul–Aug, Oct–Nov, Jan–Feb, Apr–May (FY runs April–March)
    US10-Q within 40–45 days; large caps report 2–4 weeks after quarter endApr–May, Jul–Aug, Oct–Nov, Jan–Feb

    Results come with a press release and investor presentation; the earnings call follows. Read the transcript, not just the headlines.

    Before the report#

    1. Consensus: revenue, EPS, margins and the range, not only the mean.
    2. Whisper: estimate revisions in the last 2–4 weeks and the usual beat size show what is really priced in.
    3. Key metric: the number the market cares about (NIM and asset quality for banks, constant-currency growth and deal wins for IT, volumes for FMCG).
    4. Last four calls: what problems were flagged and whether they were fixed.
    5. Setup: a stock up 30% into results has a high bar; one down 30% has a low bar.
    6. Implied move: from the options market.
    implied move ≈ ATM straddle price ÷ stock price₹1,000 stock, straddle ₹60 → about ±6%

    Results × guidance: typical reaction#

    QuarterGuidance raisedMaintainedLowered
    BeatStrongest (beat-and-raise)Mixed; hoped for a raiseFalls; future matters more
    In linePositive if the raise is meaningfulMutedNegative
    MissCan recover if the raise is credibleNegativeWorst (miss-and-lower)

    A revenue beat is worth more than an EPS beat from buybacks or tax. "Sell the news" hits stocks that ran up into results.

    Reading the call#

    Positive tellsNegative tells
    Specific, quantified demand commentary"Cautiously optimistic" and other vague phrases
    Guidance raised by more than the beatRaise exactly equal to the beat
    Volunteering good newsProblems buried in prepared remarks
    Insider buying or buybacks at current pricesInsider selling before the report
    New products or customers with numbers"Strategic review" announcements

    Analysts asking the same worry several ways means a known problem.

    Post-earnings drift and options#

    • PEAD: after big surprises, prices keep drifting in the surprise direction for 30–90 days as estimates are revised (Ball and Brown, 1968). Weaker in large, well-covered stocks; holds only about 60–65% of the time.
    • IV crush: implied volatility collapses after results, so option buyers can be right on direction and still lose.
    • Manage it: use spreads instead of naked options; compare the implied move with the stock's historical results-day moves; sell premium only with defined risk.
    • Long-term investors: a good company oversold on a small, explainable miss can be an opportunity.

    After the report#

    • Compare revenue, EPS and margins with consensus, then guidance.
    • Check the cash flow and balance sheet (often in the presentation or the half-yearly filing in India).
    • Is the market's first reaction consistent with the fundamentals?
    • Over the next 30 days: direction of estimate revisions.
    • Update the thesis: what did you learn that you didn't know?

    Interest rates#

    • Rates set the risk-free rate in WACC: higher rates lower every valuation, most of all for long-duration growth stocks.
    • They also hit business economics: interest costs, consumer credit, housing, currency.
    • Real rate = nominal − inflation; high real rates are the restrictive ones.
    • Yield curve inversion has preceded recessions by 6–18 months.
    • Credit spreads widening is an early stress signal.
    SectorRates risingRates falling
    BanksOften positive (NIM widens)Negative
    InsurersPositive (investment income)Negative
    Utilities, REITsNegative (bond proxies, debt)Positive
    High-multiple techNegative (long duration)Positive
    Real estate, autos, discretionaryNegative (credit-financed demand)Positive

    Inflation and the economic cycle#

    Inflation regimeEquity implication
    Low and stable (1–3%)Supports high multiples
    Rising (3–5%)Pricing-power companies win
    High (above 5–6%)Rate hikes, multiple compression; value over growth
    DeflationReal debt burden rises; revenues fall
    PhaseEconomyLeaders
    Early (recovery)Rates cut, credit easesFinancials, discretionary, tech
    Mid (expansion)Steady growthTech, industrials, materials
    Late (peak)Inflation up, tighteningEnergy, materials
    RecessionEarnings fallStaples, healthcare, utilities

    Inflation is the stress test for moats: only real pricing power keeps margins.

    Sector rotation cycle#

    flowchart TD
  E["Early cycle: financials, discretionary"] --> M["Mid cycle: technology, industrials"]
  M --> L["Late cycle: energy, materials"]
  L --> R["Recession: staples, healthcare, utilities"]
  R --> E

    Popularised by Sam Stovall; the pattern is real but loose. Use it to set tilts, not to make all-or-nothing switches, and never drop company quality for a macro theme.

    Top-down process#

    1. Regime: growth, inflation, policy direction, yield curve, credit spreads.
    2. Sectors: which have tailwinds in this regime?
    3. Industries: best structures within them (five forces).
    4. Stocks: best moats, management and valuations.
    5. Monitor: PMI turns, policy language, curve shape, spreads.

    Sector relative strength (sector index ÷ Nifty or S&P 500) shows rotation as it happens.

    Graham's defensive-investor criteria#

    #Criterion
    1Adequate size (avoid small, obscure companies)
    2Strong finances: current ratio ≥ 2; long-term debt ≤ net current assets
    3Earnings stability: positive earnings in each of the last 10 years
    4Dividend record: uninterrupted for at least 20 years
    5Earnings growth: at least one-third over 10 years (3-year averages at each end)
    6Moderate P/E: at most 15 × average earnings of the last 3 years
    7Moderate P/B: at most 1.5, or P/E × P/B ≤ 22.5

    Criterion 7 is where the Graham number comes from: √(22.5 × EPS × BVPS).

    Buffett–Clark statement rules of thumb#

    LineDurable advantage shows as
    Gross marginConsistently 40%+
    SG&ABelow about 30% of gross profit, and steady
    R&DLow; heavy R&D means the moat needs constant rebuilding
    DepreciationBelow about 10% of gross profit
    InterestBelow 15% of operating income
    Net marginConsistently 20%+
    EPSRising steadily over 10 years
    DebtLong-term debt repayable from 3–4 years of profit

    Peter Lynch's six categories#

    CategoryProfilePlay
    Slow growers2–4% growth, matureDividends; little upside
    Stalwarts10–12% growth, largeRecession protection; take 30–50% gains
    Fast growers20–25%+ growthBiggest winners; sell when growth stalls

    "Two-minute drill": explain why you own it, what has to happen, and the risks, in two minutes.

    Greenblatt and Dorsey#

    • Magic formula: rank stocks by earnings yield (EBIT ÷ EV) and return on capital (EBIT ÷ (net working capital + net fixed assets)); buy the top 20–30 combined ranks, hold about a year, rebalance.
    • Special situations (You Can Be a Stock Market Genius): spin-offs, restructurings, merger securities, rights offerings, bankruptcies. Forced, non-economic selling creates bargains.
    • Dorsey's five rules: do your homework; find economic moats; insist on a margin of safety; hold for the long term; know when to sell.

    Building a screen#

    ScreenExample filtersWatch out for

    TradingOverview/fundamental-analysis (applied to Indian markets) · SEBI and exchange disclosure practice

    Disclosures worth reading#

    DocumentFrequencyLook for
    Financial resultsQuarterly (45 days); annual audited (60 days)Segment data, consolidated vs standalone, exceptional items
    Shareholding patternQuarterlyPromoter, FII, DII and retail trends; pledged shares
    Annual reportYearlyRelated-party note, contingent liabilities, auditor's report, CWIP

    Credit rating rationaleOn rating actionLiquidity, debt structure, group support
    Insider trading (PIT) and SAST filingsEvent-basedPromoter buying or selling, creeping acquisitions

    Indian-market red flags#

    • Promoter pledge rising, or pledged shares a large share of the promoter stake: forced selling risk.
    • Promoter holding falling steadily without a clear reason.
    • Related-party transactions large relative to profit; loans or advances to group firms.
    • Auditor resignation mid-term (reasons must be disclosed) or a tiny auditor for a large group.
    • Qualified opinion or "emphasis of matter" in the audit report.
    • CWIP piling up for years; interest capitalised into projects.
    • Other income a large share of profit; tax rate far below peers.
    • Frequent dilution: QIPs, preferential allotments, warrants to promoters at low prices.
    • Large contingent liabilities, especially tax demands and guarantees for group companies.
    • Subsidiaries carrying the debt or losses: always check consolidated numbers.

    Accounting notes for Indian companies#

    • Ind AS is converged with IFRS: leases on the balance sheet (Ind AS 116), five-step revenue recognition (Ind AS 115), fair-value items through OCI.
    • Standalone vs consolidated: valuations should use consolidated figures unless the parent is the whole business.
    • ROCE (EBIT ÷ capital employed) is the common Indian quality metric; it is close to pre-tax ROIC.
    • Holding companies usually trade at a discount to the value of their listed stakes; value them with sum of the parts.
    • PSUs: government ownership brings dividend payouts and policy risk; minority interests can come second.
    • Tax rules for dividends and capital gains change in budgets; check the current rates.

    Valuing the Indian market#

    • Earnings yield vs bonds: compare Nifty earnings yield (1 ÷ P/E) with the 10-year G-sec yield; a wide gap in favour of bonds signals expensive equities.
    • Market cap to GDP: a rough, slow gauge of overall valuation.
    • Own history: compare index P/E and P/B with their long-run ranges, not with other countries.
    • Higher nominal growth (real growth plus inflation) supports higher multiples and higher terminal growth in rupee DCFs than in US-dollar DCFs, but so does a higher risk-free rate.
    • Flows: FPI selling with steady domestic SIP inflows has become a recurring tug of war.

    13Research workflow, sell rules and checklists

    Run these before buying and every time results come out.

    One-page investment thesis#

    1. Business: what it sells, to whom, how it makes money, in two sentences.
    2. Why it wins: the moat in one sentence, with the numbers that prove it.
    3. What the market misses: your variant view.
    4. Value: base, bull and bear values; the margin of safety at today's price.
    5. Catalysts: what will make the price converge with value, and when.
    6. Risks: the three things most likely to break the thesis.
    7. Sell triggers: written now, while calm.

    Pre-buy checklist#

    • Read 5–10 years of statements; CFO tracks profit.
    • ROIC or ROCE above the cost of capital for most years.
    • Debt manageable: net debt / EBITDA below 2–3×, coverage above 5×.
    • Moat identified and not shrinking.
    • Management: capital allocation, candour, ownership, no governance flags.
    • Earnings quality: no Beneish, Altman or working-capital warnings.
    • Valuation from DCF and multiples agree; margin of safety fits the quality.
    • Position size decided; it would not ruin you if wrong.

    Sell rules#

    Sell whenNot when
    The thesis was wrong (facts misread)The price fell but value is intact
    The business has deteriorated (moat, management, governance)A macro scare hits the whole market
    Price far exceeds a conservative valueIt is merely "up a lot"
    A clearly better opportunity needs the capitalYou are bored or impatient
    Position has grown far beyond your size limitOne weak quarter with a clear explanation

    Portfolio construction#

    • 10–25 holdings for most individual investors: concentrated enough to matter, diversified enough to survive mistakes.
    • Size by conviction and margin of safety; cap any single stock at about 5–10% of the portfolio at cost.
    • Cap sector exposure and watch hidden correlation (rate-sensitive, export-dependent, same promoter group).
    • Keep some cash for market dislocations.
    • Review each holding when results come out; re-underwrite yearly.

    Quarterly monitoring#

    • Revenue, margins and EPS against your model, not just consensus.
    • CFO, working capital days and debt trend.
    • Management commentary vs last quarter's promises.
    • Shareholding: promoter stake, pledges, institutional flows.
    • Thesis status: intact, strengthened, weakened or broken?

    Combining with technicals#

    • Fundamentals pick what to own: a watchlist of quality businesses with a valuation range.
    • Technicals help with when: buy in tranches near support or on a breakout from a base; avoid catching a stock in a stage-4 decline.
    • Risk control for traders: a chart-based stop protects against thesis errors you have not spotted yet.
