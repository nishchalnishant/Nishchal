    01What a future is: contracts, rivals, purpose and specs

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 1–4 (what a future is, futures vs forwards, options, shares, why futures exist, contract specs)

    A promise with four fixed parts#

    • A future is a standardised agreement to buy or sell a fixed quantity at a fixed price on a fixed date.
    • Underlying (RELIANCE, NIFTY, a commodity), price locked today, lot size (RELIANCE 500 shares), expiry (stock futures monthly).
    • Long (buyer) is obliged to buy and profits if price rises; short (seller) is obliged to sell and profits if it falls. Both sides are bound: unlike an option, nobody can just walk away.
    • Most traders square off before expiry in cash. Index futures settle in cash; stock futures can go to physical delivery if held to expiry.

    Standardised rice contracts traded in Japan as early as the 17th century.

    Futures vs forwards, options and shares#

    Max lossLargeLarge: the full price moveThe premium paidWhat you paid

    Match the tool to the goal: long-term ownership with dividends and votes → shares; capped, known downside → buy an option; capital-efficient directional view → a future; custom business hedge → a forward.

    Why futures exist: hedgers and speculators#

    HedgerSpeculator
    Already owns the risk?YesNo, seeks it
    GoalRemove uncertaintyChance of profit
    The future isInsuranceA bet
    OK to lose on it?Yes, if holdings gainNo
    • A fund manager fearing a one-month fall sells index futures instead of dumping the whole portfolio (tax, costs, time).
    • Opposite goals make the trade possible: risk is moved, not destroyed. The market gains liquidity and price discovery; news often shows in index futures first.
    • Futures weren't invented for gambling; most leveraged speculators lose over time.

    02Trading mechanics: orders, liquidity, direction and payoff

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 5–8 (orders, liquidity and slippage, long and short, payoff)

    Order types#

    TypeYou controlFillUse when
    MarketSpeedNear-certain, price not guaranteedYou must get in or out now
    LimitPriceOnly at your price or betterYou can wait
    Stop-loss marketTriggerAt market once triggeredThe exit must happen
    Stop-loss limitTrigger and worst priceMay not fill in a fast moveYou need a price floor
    • Intraday (MIS): closed the same day, auto square-off near the close, lighter margin. Carryforward (NRML): held overnight, full margin.
    • Order states: pending, complete, rejected (margin, price band, market closed), cancelled. The order book holds intentions; the trade book holds actual fills.

    Stops don't survive gaps#

    Long 1 RELIANCE lot from 1,318 with a stop at 1,300 (planned loss ₹9,000). The stock opens at 1,280 after bad news: the stop fills near 1,280, a ₹19,000 loss. A stop limits losses in normal trading, not across overnight gaps, so size positions for the gap, not just the stop.

    Liquidity, spread and slippage#

    round-trip spread cost ≈ spread (ticks) × tick value × lotsslippage cost ≈ (average fill − expected price) × lot size
    • One-tick spread on RELIANCE: buying at the ask and selling at the bid loses ₹25 per lot with no price change; ten round trips a day cross the spread 20 times.
    • Needing 500 when 200 are offered at 1,320.00: the other 300 fill higher. An average fill of 1,320.12 instead of 1,320.00 costs ₹60 per lot.
    • Liquidity concentrates in near-month NIFTY and BANKNIFTY and the most active stocks; far months and small stock futures have wide spreads and gaps between levels.
    • Read depth before any market order, use limits in thin books, keep size small relative to visible depth.

    Long and short#

    LongShort
    Open byBuyingSelling
    Profit whenPrice risesPrice falls
    Borrowing neededNoNo
    Can hold to expiryYesYes
    Loss grows asPrice falls (floored at zero)Price rises (no ceiling)

    Buy at 1,320 and sell at 1,360, or sell at 1,320 and buy back at 1,280: either way 40 points × 500 = ₹20,000 before costs. Unlike cash-market shorts (intraday or borrowed), a futures short is just a sell order to open. Shorting is not safer than going long; put a stop on every short.

    The straight-line payoff#

    P&L = (exit − entry) × lot size (long; negate for short)breakeven = entry price (no premium to recover)
    Move from 1,320Long 1 lotShort 1 lot
    +1+₹500−₹500
    +20+₹10,000−₹10,000
    −20−₹10,000+₹10,000

    No premium and no time decay make the payoff one symmetric diagonal. Unlike an option buyer's capped loss, a long can lose the whole ₹6.6 lakh if the stock went to zero, and a short's loss has no cap. Sketch entry, breakeven and ₹ per point before every trade.

    03Margin and money: leverage, SPAN, mark-to-market, costs and tax

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 9–12 (margin and leverage, SPAN and pledging, mark-to-market, costs and taxation)

    Margin and leverage cut both ways#

    RELIANCE moveChange in ₹6.6 lakh exposureEffect on ₹1.1 lakh margin
    +2%+₹13,000+12%
    +5%+₹33,000+30%
    −2%−₹13,000−12%
    −5%−₹33,000−30%
    ≈ −17%−₹1,10,000Margin gone
    • Margin is a good-faith deposit, not a payment; leverage is what you control per rupee posted (≈ 6× here).
    • Initial margin opens the trade; the balance must stay above maintenance or you get a margin call: add funds or cut the position, or the broker closes it at market.

    SPAN, exposure and pledged collateral#

    total margin = SPAN + exposureusable pledge margin = pledged value × (1 − haircut)₹1,00,000 of shares, 15% haircut → ≈ ₹85,000
    • SPAN is the exchange's worst-case one-day loss estimate (the bulk); exposure is an extra buffer on top. Both must be free in the account before the order goes through.
    • Margin is a moving target: it rises with volatility, before budgets, policy meetings, elections and results, and for stock futures ramps up sharply through expiry week towards delivery.
    • Pledging shares, ETFs or bonds earns collateral margin after a haircut (smaller for index funds and government bonds, larger for volatile stocks). Roughly half of margin must still be cash, and daily losses are paid only in cash.

    Mark-to-market: settled every day#

    daily MTM = (today's settlement − yesterday's) × lot size
    DaySettlementDaily MTMMargin account
    Entry1,320—₹1,30,000
    Day 11,335+₹7,500₹1,37,500
    Day 21,326−₹4,500₹1,33,000
    Day 31,300−₹13,000₹1,20,000: likely a margin call

    Gains are credited and losses debited in cash the same evening, at the official settlement price, not the last price you saw. MTM changes the timing, not the total, and limits everyone's credit risk to one day's move. Unlike shares, a futures loss is never "only on paper".

    Costs: one NIFTY lot round trip#

    ChargeRate≈ ₹ (65 × 24,000, both legs ≈ ₹31.2 lakh)
    Brokerage≈ ₹20 per order40
    STT0.05% of sell value780
    Exchange charges≈ 0.0019% of turnover≈ 57–59
    SEBI fee0.0001% of turnover3
    Stamp duty0.002% of buy value31
    GST18% of brokerage + exchange + SEBI18
    Total≈ ₹929 ≈ 14 NIFTY points

    STT dominates; flat brokerage barely matters. The first ~14 points of every NIFTY lot only cover costs, and ten round trips a day cost about ₹9,000. A 500-share stock future costs about ₹330 near ₹1,000 and ₹2,875 near ₹10,000.

    How futures profits are taxed (India)#

    • Futures P&L is non-speculative business income: reported in ITR-3 and taxed at your slab rate. No special rate, and STT gives no rebate.
    • Losses can be set off against most other income in the same year except salary; unabsorbed losses carry forward 8 years, but only if the return is filed on time.
    • For the tax-audit test, turnover ≈ sum of absolute profits and losses on each trade.
    • Advance tax instalments apply if your tax crosses the threshold; thresholds and rates change, so confirm with a chartered accountant.

    04Pricing and the cycle: basis, expiry, rollover and spreads

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 13–16 (basis and carry, expiry calendar, rollover, calendar spreads and basis)

    Basis and cost of carry#

    basis = futures − spotfair future ≈ spot + spot × financing rate × days ÷ 3651,318 × 7% × 32 ÷ 365 ≈ 8 → fair future ≈ 1,326
    StateFuture vs spotUsual meaning
    PremiumAboveNormal carry; calm to mildly bullish
    DiscountBelowA big dividend, or bearish positioning
    Near zeroCloseExpiry near; carry nearly gone

    The premium widens with time and rates and shrinks towards expiry; at settlement the basis must reach zero (convergence). A long future loses the premium even if spot stays flat. Always ask why the gap is that size: carry, dividend or sentiment.

    The expiry calendar#

    ContractLiquidity
    Near monthHigh: tight spreads, trade here
    Next monthModerate; busy during rollover
    Far monthThin: wide spreads
    • Three monthly contracts trade at once; when the near month expires, a new far month is listed.
    • NSE: the last Tuesday of the month (moves to the previous trading day on a holiday); NIFTY weeklies every Tuesday, so the last Tuesday has both and is often the wildest session. BSE: Thursday.
    • From 3 Aug 2026, equity derivatives trade until 15:40; the daily settlement price is the 15:10–15:40 VWAP. Final settlement of stock futures uses the underlying's closing-auction price; index futures use the official closing index value.

    Rollover#

    roll cost per share = next-month price − near-month pricesell July 1,322, buy August 1,334 → 12 × 500 = ₹6,000
    • Long roll: sell near, buy next. Short roll: buy back near, sell next. Same size and direction; only the month changes. Longs pay the spread, shorts collect it.
    • Roll in the days before expiry in liquid contracts, both legs close together.
    • Rollover % (share of expiring open interest carried forward): high suggests conviction, low suggests hesitation. A soft hint, never a signal on its own.
    • Never let a stock future drift into settlement: holding one RELIANCE lot to expiry means taking or giving 500 shares (≈ ₹6.6 lakh).

    Expiry is coming: close or roll?#

    A futures position with expiry in the next few days
    1Do you still want this exposure next month?
    no
    Close itbefore expiry week; stock futures must never drift into delivery
    yes ↓
    2Is the next month liquid yet, in the rollover window?
    no
    Wait for the rollover windowthin next-month quotes cost slippage
    yes ↓
    3Is the roll cost small relative to the move you expect?
    no
    Close insteadthe carry eats the expected gain
    yes
    Rollplace both legs together, same size and direction

    Calendar spreads and basis trades#

    • Calendar spread: long one expiry, short another of the same underlying. Direction mostly cancels; the gap between months drives P&L. A 40-point move that makes ₹20,000 on an outright lot barely moves the spread. Not risk-free: the gap can move against you.
    • Cash-futures arbitrage: spot 1,318, future 1,338 is a 20-point premium against ~8 points of fair carry. Buy 500 shares (full ₹6.6 lakh cash), sell one future, hold to expiry, and the gap converges to zero. Costs and spreads can eat a few points, so this is for large, cheap capital, not retail accounts.
    • Arbitrageurs are what keep futures tethered to fair value.

    05Settlement and positioning: delivery vs cash, index vs stock, open interest

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 17–19 (settlement, index vs stock futures, open interest)

    Stock delivery vs index cash#

    Stock futures (RELIANCE)Index futures (NIFTY, BANKNIFTY)
    Final settlementPhysical deliveryCash difference
    Held to expiryLong pays ≈ ₹6.6 lakh and gets 500 shares; short must deliver 500 sharesSettled in cash: 100 points × 65 = ±₹6,500
    Full value neededYesNo
    Accidental deliveryPossibleImpossible
    • The exchange fixes the method per contract class; you can't choose.
    • Daily MTM (funds settlement) is always cash and separate from final settlement.
    • Stock futures carry a delivery margin that ramps up in stages through expiry week: treat it as a deadline to close, roll or fund delivery.
    • A short that can't deliver is bought in at auction, with costs and penalties on the seller.

    Index vs stock futures#

    FeatureIndexStock
    UnderlyingA diversified basketOne company
    Lot (per course)NIFTY 65, BANKNIFTY 30RELIANCE 500
    SettlementCashPhysical
    Single-company newsDilutedLands entirely on you
    LiquidityVery deepGood in large names, thin in smaller ones
    Overnight gap riskLowerHigh

    Index futures are deeper, smoother and cash-settled: the better place to learn and to hedge. Stock futures suit a specific, well-researched view on one company, with respect for gaps.

    Open interest: price plus OI#

    PriceOINameRead
    UpUpLong build-upStrong: fresh buyers
    UpDownShort coveringWeak: shorts closing
    DownUpShort build-upStrong downtrend: fresh sellers
    DownDownLong unwindingWeak: longs exiting
    • OI counts open contracts (each once, though it has a buyer and a seller). New buyer meets new seller: OI rises; both close: it falls; a position changes hands: unchanged.
    • Volume resets daily; OI carries forward. OI data is usually end of day.
    • In rollover week near-month OI falls as next-month OI rises.
    • OI shows conviction, not direction: a confirming signal, never a standalone trigger.

    06Market controls: position limits, bans, adjustments and halts

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 20–22 (position limits and ban, corporate actions, price bands and halts)

    Position limits and the F&O ban#

    • MWPL (market-wide position limit) caps total open positions in one stock's F&O, as a share of free float. Client-level and member-level limits cap individuals and brokers.
    • Ban: when MWPL use reaches about 95%, the stock enters the F&O ban; it exits when use falls to about 80%.
    • During a ban you may only reduce or square off: no fresh longs or shorts, no adding, even with enough margin. Closing-only trading on thin two-way interest can swing sharply.
    • The exchange publishes the ban list daily; check it before every single-stock trade.
    • Quantity freeze: orders above a maximum size are rejected; split them.

    Corporate actions: value-neutral rescaling#

    adjustment factor = new shares per old sharenew lot = old lot × factor; new strike = old strike ÷ factor
    RELIANCE 2:1 splitBeforeAfter
    Share price≈ ₹1,320≈ ₹660
    Lot size5001,000
    Lot value≈ ₹6.6 lakh≈ ₹6.6 lakh
    1,320 call strike1,320660
    • Applied from the ex-date automatically: no order, fee, gain or loss.
    • Bonus works like a split (1:1 bonus = factor 2). Ordinary dividends: usually no adjustment. Special dividends: adjusted only above an exchange threshold. Rights, mergers and demergers: case by case per exchange circular.

    Price bands, flexing and circuit breakers#

    • Every contract has a daily operating range around the reference price; orders outside it are rejected, not parked. RELIANCE near ₹1,318 with a ~10% band: a buy at ₹1,470 is rejected.
    • Dynamic bands in F&O: price pressing the edge triggers a short pause, then the band widens by a step (often ~5%), possibly several times a day.
    • Market-wide breakers at roughly 10%, 15% and 20% moves in NIFTY or SENSEX halt cash and derivatives together; halt length depends on the level and time of day, and a 20% move ends trading for the day. Reopening uses a call auction, so prices can gap.
    • "A stop can only work if the market is open and trading at your level." A position locked at the lower band may not be sellable for hours: size so that is survivable.

    07Risk and discipline: hedging, sizing, gaps and leverage

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Futures Trading · chapters 23–27 (hedging, position sizing, gap and event risk, risks of leverage, mistakes and checklist)

    Hedging a portfolio with index futures#

    lots ≈ (portfolio value × beta) ÷ (index level × lot size)₹15 lakh × 1.0 ÷ (24,000 × 65 = ₹15.6 lakh) ≈ 0.96 → short 1 lot
    NIFTY movesPortfolioShort futureNet
    −5%≈ −₹75,000≈ +₹78,000≈ flat
    +5%≈ +₹75,000≈ −₹78,000Upside given up
    • Beta scales the hedge: above 1 needs more lots (round up), below 1 fewer.
    • A hedge is temporary insurance for a specific, time-bound risk; its premium is capped upside plus running costs. Permanent hedges erode returns, and sometimes trimming shares is simpler.

    Position sizing: loss first, then lots#

    risk budget = 1–2% of trading capital (₹5 lakh × 1% = ₹5,000)loss per lot = stop distance × ₹ per pointlots = budget ÷ loss per lot, rounded DOWN
    SetupLoss per lotVerdict
    NIFTY, 30-point stop × ₹65₹1,950Raw 2.5 → take 1 lot after costs
    RELIANCE, ₹8 stop × 500₹4,0001 lot
    RELIANCE, ₹10 stop × 500₹5,000Exactly the budget, no cushion
    RELIANCE, ₹12 stop × 500₹6,000Over budget: no trade

    Never size by the margin that lets you in. The stop comes from the chart first; a wider stop means fewer lots. At 1% per loss, six losses are a 6% drawdown; at 20%, two losses cripple the account. For many small accounts, one lot is the prudent maximum.

    Gap and event risk#

    RELIANCE long, close 1,318, stop 1,290Loss (500 sh)Share of ₹1.1 lakh margin
    Planned stop exit≈ ₹14,000≈ 13%
    Opens at 1,250, stop fills there≈ ₹34,000≈ 31%
    6% gap down≈ ₹39,500Over a third
    10% gap down≈ ₹66,000Over half
    • Gap triggers: results after the close, RBI policy, the Union Budget, election counting, overseas news while India is shut.
    • Exchanges also raise margins before such events, which can force you out before the news lands.
    • Check the event calendar before carrying anything overnight; cut size or go flat, keep a cash buffer, stay in liquid near-month contracts, and size so a gap through the stop is survivable.

    The margin-call spiral#

    flowchart TD
  L["Adverse move or gap"] --> MS["Margin falls below maintenance"]
  MS --> MC["Margin call"]
  MC --> FX["Forced exit at a poor price"]
  FX --> SC["Smaller capital"]
  SC --> BB["Bigger bets to win it back"]
  BB --> L

    "Most people who trade leveraged futures lose money." With ≈ 6× leverage a 5% move is ≈ 30% of margin either way, and an 8% gap is nearly half. Leverage, not the instrument, is the danger: keep risk per trade small and fixed and let the stop distance choose the size.

    Beginner mistakes and their fixes#

    MistakeFix
    Sizing off margin, not exposureSize off full contract value (≈ ₹6.6 lakh per RELIANCE future)
    Trading without a stopSet the exit price before entry
    Averaging into a loserNever add to a losing leveraged position
    Trading the far monthStay in the near month
    Over-trading, ignoring costsCount round-trip costs (≈ 14 NIFTY points) in every target
    Holding stock futures to expiryClose or roll before the final days
    Revenge tradingFollow the pre-written plan after losses
    Treating margin as the maximum lossMargin is a deposit; gaps can lose more than it

    08Pre-trade checklist and one-line formulas

    Run this on every futures trade until it is automatic.

    Futures pre-trade checklist#

    1. Contract: exact symbol, near month, expiry date noted; not in the F&O ban.
    2. Margin: calculator checked; cash buffer above it for hikes and MTM.
    3. Size: 1–2% of capital at risk, from full exposure and stop distance, rounded down.
    4. Stop: set before entry, beyond the invalidation level.
    5. Costs: round-trip cost and breakeven points known.
    6. Events: results, RBI, budget, elections, expiry week checked; reduce or flatten ahead of shocks.
    7. Exit plan: target and stop written; no averaging down; stock futures closed or rolled before delivery.
