    01Why and what: rights, jobs, rivals and the two building blocks

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Basics · chapters 1–4 (what an option is, why options exist, options vs futures vs stocks, calls and puts)

    A right, not an obligation#

    • An option gives its buyer the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell an underlying at a fixed strike on or before a fixed expiry.
    • The buyer pays a premium up front. That premium is the most the buyer can lose: if the deal turns bad, they simply walk away.
    • The seller pockets the premium and takes on the duty to honour the deal.
    • Token-money picture: pay ₹1 lakh to lock a ₹50 lakh flat for 3 months. If prices jump to ₹60 lakh, buy at ₹50 lakh; if they fall or plans change, walk away and lose only the ₹1 lakh. Token = premium, ₹50 lakh = strike, 3 months = expiry, flat = underlying.

    Test for any trade: "Am I paying a small, known amount today to lock a price, with the freedom to walk away later?"

    The four jobs options do#

    JobTypical toolRisk carried
    Hedge a holdingBuy a putPremium paid
    Speculate on directionBuy a call or putPremium paid
    Earn incomeSell a call or putLarge, sometimes unlimited
    LeverageBuy a call or putPremium paid
    • Three of the four jobs are buyer jobs with a capped loss. Only selling carries open-ended risk.
    • ₹15,590 buys upside on 500 RELIANCE shares worth ≈ ₹6.59 lakh, about 1/42 of the share cost.
    • Institutions mostly use options to manage risk. A common retail pattern is buying cheap, far out-of-the-money options as lottery tickets, which usually decay to nothing.
    • Name the job before you trade. If it is none of the four, it is a punt.

    Stock vs future vs long call#

    FeatureStock (500 shares)Future (1 lot)Long 1320 call (1 lot)
    Cash up front≈ ₹6.59 lakhMargin, often ≈ 1/5 of value≈ ₹15,590 premium
    Payoff shapeStraight line, ₹500 per ₹1Straight line on full exposureBent: flat loss below strike, rises above breakeven
    Daily mark-to-market and margin callsNoYesNo
    Time decayNoNoYes, every day
    ExpiryNoneFixedFixed
    Worst caseWhole value, only if the company goes to zeroNo cap; can exceed the depositPremium paid

    An option is not "a cheaper future". The two have different risk shapes: the future gives the full straight-line move with no floor on loss, while the long option caps the loss but leaks value with time. The same correct view can win in one instrument and lose in another, so match the instrument to your timing, risk tolerance and the cash you can afford to lose.

    Calls and puts: the two building blocks#

    Call (CE)Put (PE)
    Right toBuy at the strikeSell at the strike
    Buyer wantsA meaningful riseA meaningful fall, or insurance on shares
    At 1400 / 1250 (1320 strike)Valuable / worthlessWorthless / valuable
    • Memory aid: call up, put down. You "call" shares in to buy them and "put" them away to sell.
    • At a near-spot strike the call and put cost about the same (≈ ₹31 each here): the market sees up and down as roughly equally likely, so choosing between them is a directional call, not a bargain hunt.
    • Indian exchange-traded options are European style: they settle at expiry, but you can close them any time before by trading the opposite side.
    • Every strategy is a mix of bought and sold calls and puts. Shares plus a bought put is protection; a call plus a put profits from a big move either way; a sold option earns premium in a quiet market.

    02The contract: premium, strike, expiry and settlement

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Basics · chapters 5–8 (premium, strike, expiry, right, not obligation, index vs stock options, expiry, exercise and assignment)

    Premium, strike and expiry#

    NumberWhat it isChanges?
    PremiumPrice of the option, quoted per share and paid per lot; the buyer's max lossEvery tick
    StrikeLocked price: calls buy at it, puts sell at itNever
    ExpiryDeadline after which the right vanishesNever
    • A strike nearer spot, or a later expiry, means a higher premium. Each strike on the ladder (1300, 1320, 1340…) is a separate option with its own price.
    • Time works against the buyer and for the seller. Time value erodes faster as expiry nears.
    • NSE options (RELIANCE, NIFTY, BANKNIFTY) expire on Tuesdays; monthly = last Tuesday. BSE derivatives such as SENSEX expire on Thursdays.

    Reading an option symbol#

    RELIANCE 28JUL26 1320 CEunderlying · expiry (day, month, year) · strike · typeCE = call, PE = put
    • RELIANCE28JUL261320PE is the matching put: only the last two letters differ.
    • Mixing up CE and PE in a fast market is a classic beginner slip; read the last two letters before every order.
    • Cost check: premium per share × lot = what leaves your account.

    Buyer's right, seller's duty#

    Buyer (holder)Seller (writer)
    CashPays the premiumReceives the premium
    GetsA right to exercise or walk awayA duty if the buyer exercises (assignment)
    Best caseOpen-endedKeeps the premium
    Worst casePremium lostLarge or unlimited
    MarginNone beyond the premiumMust post margin
    Time decayAgainstFor
    • The buyer exercises only when the strike beats the open market; otherwise the option expires worthless.
    • 1320 call, premium 31: at 1400 exercise (buy ₹80 below market); at 1290, 1200 or 1000 the loss is the same capped ₹15,590.
    • The seller's premium is pay for carrying risk, like an insurer's, not a gift. Capped loss does not mean easy profit either: most call buyers miss breakeven in time.

    Index vs stock options in India#

    UnderlyingFamilyLot (course)In the money at expiry
    NIFTYIndex (weekly + monthly)65Cash payout
    BANKNIFTYIndex30Cash payout
    FINNIFTYIndex60Cash payout
    MIDCPNIFTYIndex120Cash payout
    RELIANCESingle stock (monthly)500Physical delivery of 500 shares
    • An index has no shares behind it, so it settles in rupees. A stock option held in the money to expiry becomes a real trade: a 1320 call bought for ₹15,590 can turn into a bill of 500 × 1320 = ₹6.6 lakh; a short put can force you to buy shares.
    • Exercised options pay STT of 0.15% on the intrinsic (settlement) value from 1 Apr 2026, much more than the STT on simply selling the option.
    • Stock options also face the F&O ban, corporate-action adjustments to lot and strike, quantity freezes and price bands.
    • Safe habit: square off stock options before expiry week unless you intend to give or take delivery with full funds. With index options, still manage your own exit.

    No partial credit: finishing ₹1 out of the money is worth the same as ₹100 out, which is zero.

    Auto-exercise, assignment and the settlement price#

    • At expiry time value is gone and only intrinsic value settles. 1320 call settling at 1360 pays ₹40 a share ≈ ₹20,000 a lot; at 1320 or 1290 it pays nothing.
    • The exchange auto-exercises every in-the-money option. A seller of that option is assigned and must settle it; the premium collected does not cap that duty.
    • Index options settle on the index's official close. For stocks, the course notes that from 3 Aug 2026 the official close normally comes from the closing auction, with the 15:00–15:15 VWAP as reference and fallback; stock options trade until 15:40 and the derivative closing price uses the 15:10–15:40 VWAP.
    • You cannot influence the settlement number. Square off in time to avoid delivery, exercise STT and surprise fills.

    Rules change: confirm the current NSE contract specification and NSE Clearing schedule before any expiry.

    03Reading the price: moneyness, value split, live P&L, chain and spread

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Basics · chapters 9–13 (moneyness, intrinsic and time value, payoff vs live P&L, the option chain, bid, ask, spread and liquidity)

    In, at and out of the money#

    Strike (spot 1,318)CallPut
    1260, 1300ITMOTM
    1320ATMATM
    1340, 1380OTMITM
    • Test: exercise this second, would you gain? Yes is ITM, no is OTM, on the line is ATM.
    • Calls want a low strike (buy cheap); puts want a high strike (sell dear). 1300 call: ₹18 intrinsic. 1340 put: ₹22 intrinsic.
    • Moneyness moves with price: at 1,360 the 1320 call is ₹40 ITM and the put is OTM; at 1,280 the reverse.
    • OTM options are cheap because they are pure hope; cheap is not good value. And ITM is not the same as profitable: it still has to cover the premium paid.

    Intrinsic value plus time value#

    premium = intrinsic value + time valuecall intrinsic = max(spot − strike, 0)put intrinsic = max(strike − spot, 0)time value = premium − intrinsic
    • Spot 1,318, both 1320 options ≈ ₹31: call = 0 intrinsic + 31 time; put = 2 intrinsic + 29 time.
    • Time value is the "hope premium". It melts every day, slowly at first and steeply in the final week, and is zero at expiry.
    • A flat stock still costs the buyer: two flat weeks at 1,318 shrink both premiums.
    • Before buying, ask how far, and how fast, the stock must move to beat that melt.

    Payoff at expiry vs live P&L#

    Line on the chartShowsShaped by
    Solid, bent lineP&L if held to expiry: pure intrinsic valueStrike and premium only
    Dotted, rounded curve (T+0)What the position is worth todayDelta, theta, vega, and the bid-ask spread
    Gap between themTime value still leftShrinks to nothing by expiry morning
    • Example: next day RELIANCE goes 1,318 → 1,325, the right way. Delta ≈ 0.5 passes on only ≈ ₹3.5 of the ₹7 move; a day of theta and the spread (you bought at the ask, you are valued at the bid) take their share, so the P&L can be near flat or slightly negative.
    • Falling implied volatility (vega) can shrink the premium even when spot does not move.
    • Keep two questions apart: "Was I right on direction?" and "Am I in profit?" Use the payoff chart as your expiry plan; track the trade on the today curve.

    Reading the option chain#

    Call LTPCall OIStrikePut LTPPut OI
    421,80,0001300242,40,000
    361,30,0001310281,60,000
    313,10,0001320 (ATM)313,40,000
    261,50,0001330371,20,000
    212,00,00013404490,000
    • Layout: calls on the left, strikes down the middle, puts on the right; one row is one strike for one expiry. Switching expiry reprices everything, and later expiries cost more.
    • Find ATM first: the strike nearest spot. Calls get cheaper up the strikes, puts cheaper down them.
    • Fields: LTP last trade · bid best buyer · ask best seller · volume today's trades · OI contracts still open.
    • Deep ITM strikes are dear because of intrinsic value; far OTM strikes are cheap because they need big moves. Heavy OI marks where traders are positioned: a clue, not a forecast. The chain prices rights; it does not predict direction. (Illustrative, rounded numbers from the course.)

    Bid, ask, spread and liquidity#

    RELIANCE callBidAskSpread per lot
    1320 (ATM)30.8031.20₹0.40 ≈ ₹200
    1500 (far OTM)0.501.50₹1.00 ≈ ₹500
    • You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, always the worse side. Buying and instantly selling loses the spread with no move at all.
    • On the 1500 strike you pay 1.50 and could sell for 0.50: about two-thirds of the premium gone to spread.
    • LTP is a past trade and can be stale on sleepy strikes. Size off the live bid and ask.
    • Liquidity filter before any idea: tight spread, real volume and open interest. Wide spread plus thin volume means walk away. Prefer near-ATM strikes on major underlyings in the nearer expiries.

    04The four payoffs and who has the edge

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Basics · chapters 14–17 (long call, long put, short call and put, buyer vs seller)

    The four payoffs side by side#

    Position (1320 strike, premium ≈ 31, lot 500)WantsMax profitMax lossBreakevenTime decay
    Long callA big rise, soonNo ceilingPremium ≈ ₹15,5901351 = strike + premiumAgainst
    Long putA big fall, soonLarge: price can't go below 0Premium ≈ ₹15,5901289 = strike − premiumAgainst
    Short callStay below 1320Premium ≈ ₹15,590Unlimited1351For
    Short putStay above 1320Premium ≈ ₹15,590Very large: ≈ ₹6.44 lakh if the stock went to 01289For

    Each ₹1 beyond breakeven is worth ₹500 to the lot. Buyers and sellers of the same option are exact mirror images: one side's capped loss is the other side's capped gain.

    Long call: buy the right to buy#

    • Payoff at expiry: a flat loss of the premium below 1320, a hinge at the strike, zero at 1351, then a climb with no ceiling.
    • Three endings: 1310 → lose ≈ ₹15,590 · 1351 → about zero · 1400 → (1400 − 1351) × 500 ≈ ₹24,500 profit.
    • The stock must clear breakeven, not just the strike. A move to 1345 helps a shareholder but still loses for this call buyer.
    • The whole premium here is time value, which leaks daily and fastest in the final week. Be right on size and timing, not only direction.
    • Makes sense when you expect a sizeable move reasonably soon and risk only money you can lose entirely. A call that needs a miracle by Friday is usually a donation.

    Long put: profit from a fall, or insure shares#

    • Mirror of the long call: worthless above 1320, breakeven 1289, then ₹500 per ₹1 fall. At 1250: (1289 − 1250) × 500 ≈ ₹19,500.
    • At expiry: value = (strike − price) × lot when positive; net P&L = that value − premium.
    • Right but still losing: a fall to 1295 is real, yet above breakeven, so the trade loses after decay.
    • Protective put: own 500 shares (≈ ₹6.59 lakh) and buy one 1320 put for ≈ ₹15,590. A crash to 1200 is mostly offset by the put; a rise to 1400 lets the put lapse. Premium = insurance cost, strike = floor, expiry = when the policy ends.

    Short call and short put#

    • Selling (writing) an option: receive the premium now, take on the obligation. The best case is that it expires worthless and you keep everything.
    • Short call wants the stock below the strike; above 1351 every ₹1 costs ₹500, without limit. At 1400 the loss is ≈ ₹24,500.
    • Short put wants the stock above the strike; below 1289 every ₹1 costs ₹500, down toward zero.
    • Sellers post margin (SPAN for a worst-case day plus an exposure buffer), far larger than the premium, and it can rise as the trade goes against you. Check it in a margin calculator first.
    • Cap the tail: own the shares behind a sold call, or pair the sold option with a bought one (a spread).

    The seller's trap in numbers#

    6 calm months selling the 1320 call ≈ 6 × ₹15,590 ≈ ₹93,540one gap to 1450: (1450 − 1351) × 500 ≈ ₹49,500 loss→ over 3 months of premium gone in one move

    Selling produces many small wins and rare large losses. A seller can win 9 trades out of 10 and still finish down if the tenth loss is bigger than the nine wins combined. Premium received is rented-out risk, not earned income.

    Buyer vs seller: who has the edge?#

    BuyerSeller
    RiskCapped at premiumLarge or unlimited
    RewardLarge or unlimitedCapped at premium
    Typical win rateLow: most options expire worthlessHigh
    Time decayDaily enemyDaily friend
    Must be right onDirection, size and timingNot being hit by the tail
    SuitsA specific, time-bound view with an affordable lossTraders who can fund margin and cap the tail

    "Buyers have limited risk; sellers have the odds." Neither is a free lunch: the premium is where the two trades meet. Win rate and win size are separate measures, so weigh both. The edge comes from judgement, sizing and discipline, not from the choice of side. Before any trade, say that side's catch out loud.

    05Volatility and positioning: Greeks, IV, expected move, OI and PCR

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Basics · chapters 18–21 (the Greeks, implied volatility, HV, IV and the expected move, open interest, volume and PCR)

    The Greeks: four plain questions#

    GreekQuestion it answersTypical valuesBuyer's experience
    DeltaHow much does the premium move per ₹1 in the stock?Calls 0 to +1, puts 0 to −1; ATM ≈ ±0.5, deep ITM near 1, far OTM near 0The profit engine when you are right
    GammaHow fast does delta itself change?Highest ATM, especially near expiry; low deep ITM and far OTMSudden speed-up near the strike late in the cycle
    ThetaHow much value leaks per day from time alone?1320 call ≈ ₹1 a day ≈ ₹500 per lot; largest ATM; brutal in the final weekThe daily enemy (sellers collect it)
    VegaHow much does the premium move when implied volatility changes?Highest ATM with plenty of time leftHidden gains or losses with a flat stock
    • Delta also gives a rough feel for the chance of finishing in the money: 0.15 suggests slim odds. It is a feel, not an exact probability, and it moves as the stock moves (that is gamma).
    • A flat week can still cost the buyer twice: theta bleeds value and a dip in implied volatility (vega) takes more.
    • Read delta and theta together before buying. A position is the sum of all four Greeks, not one of them. OpenAlgo can report Greeks for any strike.

    Implied volatility: the price of fear#

    Mood (RELIANCE 1320 call, all else fixed)IVPremium
    Calm≈ 15%Below ₹31
    Normal (anchor)≈ 20%≈ ₹31
    Nervous, news due≈ 35%Well above
    Fearful, event looming50%+2–3× the anchor
    • Implied volatility is the market's expected future movement, quoted as an annual %, backed out of option prices. It looks forward; it is not a record of past moves.
    • High IV means expensive options, low IV cheap ones. IV says how costly the bet is, never which way it goes.
    • Cheap, low-IV options are cheap because a small move is expected, and it may not come.

    IV crush around events#

    flowchart TD
  C["Calm: IV near its usual range"] --> B["Event approaches: demand lifts IV, premiums swell"]
  B --> R["Result lands: the uncertainty is resolved"]
  R --> K["IV crush: premium falls toward intrinsic value"]
  K --> N["Normal again: price reflects the real move and the time left"]

    To win as a buyer, the real move must beat the move the crowd already paid for. Sellers aim to collect the swollen premium and keep it as IV falls, which carries large risk.

    HV vs IV, and IV rank#

    IV rank = (IV now − 1-yr low) / (1-yr high − 1-yr low) × 100RELIANCE: (20 − 14) / (34 − 14) = 30% → lower half of its yearIV percentile = % of past-year days with IV below today's
    • Historical (realised) volatility is what the stock actually did over a past window, say 20–30 trading days. IV is what is priced in through expiry.
    • IV well above HV: the market is paying for a move not yet seen (results due?). IV below HV: options look cheap next to recent movement.
    • Volatility is relative: 20% is calm for one stock and stormy for another. Never judge IV alone; check its rank or percentile first. Expensive is not automatically bad, and cheap is not automatically good.

    Expected move from the ATM straddle#

    NIFTY, 28 Jul monthlyValue
    Spot / ATM strike≈ 24,056 / 24,050
    ATM call + ATM put≈ 426 + 295
    Straddle = expected move≈ ±720 points
    Expected range≈ 23,336 – 24,776
    • The straddle price is a rough one-standard-deviation band: the close lands inside it about two-thirds of the time. It is neither a guarantee nor a direction call.
    • Right and still losing: buy a NIFTY call, NIFTY rises 300 points. Less than half the priced 720-point move arrived, so the time value paid for it is not recovered.
    • For sellers, the straddle is the cushion they hope price stays inside.

    Open interest, volume and PCR#

    PriceOIRead asMeaning
    UpUpLong build-upFresh buyers entering
    UpDownShort coveringSellers buying back; can fade once done
    DownUpShort build-upFresh sellers entering
    DownDownLong unwindingBuyers exiting
    • Volume counts trades today and resets each morning. Open interest counts contracts still open and carries over. Two new traders opening: OI +1. One closing to a new one: OI unchanged. Both closing: OI −1. Volume rises in every case.
    • Read the change in OI together with price, never the OI level alone. Build-up moves are usually seen as better supported, but that is a soft judgement.
    • OI walls: heavy put OI below price is often called support and heavy call OI above it resistance. These are tendencies that break often, especially on trend and event days.
    • PCR = total put OI ÷ total call OI. Above 1 is read as bearish positioning or, if crowded, contrarian bullish; below 1 the reverse. No fixed threshold works and the same reading is argued both ways: it is a mood gauge.
    • All of these describe positioning, not intent: a put block may be a bet, a hedge or one leg of a spread. Use them as context alongside price and a plan, never as the reason to enter.

    Delta roughly tracks the odds of finishing in the money (0.80 ≈ 80% feel, 0.15 ≈ one in seven). Match delta to your confidence and the size of move you expect. Buying many long shots does not improve the odds; it multiplies small losses.

    Seller margin, hedging and pledging#

    • A naked short index option blocks roughly ₹1.5–1.8 lakh of margin per lot: SPAN (loss across a range of moves) plus an exposure buffer. A few thousand rupees of premium can sit on a block many times its size.
    • Hedge to cut margin: buy a further-out option against the one sold. The worst case becomes bounded, so margin often drops to a small fraction of the naked figure. Hedging lowers both risk and margin.
    • Pledging shares or certain funds adds collateral, after a haircut. At least about half the margin must still be cash, and mark-to-market losses are settled in cash, so keep a cash buffer or risk a forced exit or a penalty.

    Thin scalps lose to charges#

    Buy 1320 call at 31, lot 500Case A, sell at 31.50: gross ₹250; STT 0.15% × ₹15,750 ≈ ₹24 all charges ≈ ₹80–100 → net ≈ ₹150Case B, sell at 31.10: gross ₹50 − same charges → net loss

    Breakeven = strike + premium (1351) only before costs; the true breakeven sits a little higher. Judge every trade by its net result after the full stack, not by the premium move.

    Event risk: results, RBI policy and the Budget#

    EventTypically moves
    Company results (e.g. RELIANCE quarterlies)That stock and its options
    RBI monetary policyBanks, rate-sensitive stocks, the index
    Union BudgetWhole sectors in one session
    ElectionsThe index, sharply and in either direction
    Global, e.g. a US Fed meetingNIFTY via risk sentiment
    • The week before results the same 1320 call can cost ≈ ₹50 instead of ≈ ₹31, with no stock move at all.
    • Right and still losing: buy at ≈ ₹50 with the stock at 1318; results lift it to 1345 (₹25 intrinsic), but after the crush the premium is ≈ ₹35. Loss ≈ ₹15 a share ≈ ₹7,500 a lot, because the real move was smaller than the one priced in.
    • Naked selling into a binary event is not a beginner trade; a surprise beyond the priced range can exceed the premium many times. If you sell, define the risk with a bought option.
    • Habits: check the calendar against your expiry, position while IV is calm, or wait until after the crush when premiums are cheaper.

    07Trading safely: sizing, defined risk, mistakes and spreads

    OpenAlgo Open Varsity · Options Basics · chapters 25–26 (risk and the classic mistakes, spreads and checklist)

    Size by premium at risk#

    premium at risk per trade ≈ 2–5% of capital (decided before entry)one RELIANCE 1320 lot ≈ ₹15,590on ₹2 lakh: 1 lot ≈ 8% · 3 lots ≈ 25% on one bet
    • A bought option can go to zero, so the premium is the amount at risk. Decide the loss you accept first, then work out lots.
    • On a ₹2 lakh account even one lot of this option is above the 2–5% band: trade a cheaper contract or skip it.
    • If you cannot state the maximum loss in one sentence, do not enter. Long option: the premium. Naked short call: effectively unlimited. Naked short put: very large.

    Stop-loss vs defined risk#

    • Stops can gap straight past your level, especially on illiquid strikes and around events; a fill is never guaranteed.
    • Defined risk means the position itself fixes the worst case. A bought option is the simplest example; a spread caps a sold option with a bought one.
    • Prefer structure over stop orders that must execute in a fast market: capped risk cannot be widened by a gap.
    • Revenge trades (bigger, faster bets to win a loss back) are usually oversized. Expiry day is the worst time for them: steepest theta, cheap OTM temptation, weakest judgement. When the urge appears, stop for the day.

    Classic mistakes and their fixes#

    MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
    Far-OTM lottery buyingMostly time value and hope; most expire worthlessChoose delta for your expected move, not the price tag
    Averaging downAdds to a decaying asset that can still go to zeroSize once; exit at the written loss limit
    Holding blindly into expiryOTM goes to zero; ITM stock options can mean delivery worth lakhsSquare off in time
    Ignoring IV crushInflated pre-event premium collapses after the newsCheck the calendar and IV rank
    Illiquid strikesOverpay to enter, poor or no exit in a fast marketTight spread, real volume and OI only
    Naked selling without knowing marginSmall credit, open-ended risk; one gap erases monthsDefine the risk with a spread
    Judging trades by premiumCharges turn thin wins into lossesCount the full charges stack

    Almost every classic mistake is a risk failure (size, liquidity, time, emotion), not a wrong call on direction. Survival beats prediction.

    When not to trade options#

    • You can't state the maximum loss and the breakeven after charges.
    • You are trying to recover a recent loss.
    • The strike is illiquid: wide spread, thin volume and OI.
    • A known event is about to crush IV and you would be buying inflated premium.
    • It is expiry afternoon and cheap OTM tickets are tempting you.
    • You would sell naked without understanding margin, assignment and delivery.
    • The money is needed for living costs, loans or anything you cannot lose.

    A leg is one option; a spread combines legs. Selling one option and buying a further one as a backstop makes the loss known before entry and cuts margin sharply. Named strategies (verticals, straddles, iron condors) are in the Options Strategies sheet.

    08Checklists and quick reference

    Run the checklist before every order, single leg or spread.

    Eight-point pre-trade checklist#

    1. Direction and date: up, down or flat, and by when. A view without a date is not a trade.
    2. Strike: matches the view; far OTM is mostly hope.
    3. Expiry: the clock you rent; near is cheap but decays fast.
    4. Liquidity: tight bid-ask, real volume and OI.
    5. Maximum loss: a rupee figure. Bought option = the full premium.
    6. Breakeven after charges: strike ± premium, pushed further out by costs.
    7. Event risk: results, policy or Budget before expiry?
    8. Exit plan: profit target and loss limit written down, e.g. close if the premium doubles or halves, whichever comes first.
